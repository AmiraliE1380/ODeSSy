\begin{abstract}
Aggressive compiler optimization today occupies two distant extremes.
Conventional optimizers run in milliseconds but reason through ad-hoc
heuristics that miss opportunities; superoptimizers synthesize faster code
but take hours, confining them to small kernels and offline use. We adopt and extend
\emph{super-analysis}, a middle ground that uses an SMT solver purely for
\emph{analysis} while transformations remain conventional, avoiding
program synthesis entirely. The key argument for super-analysis is that conventional compilers have rich transformations rules, but proving them to be safe requires complex reasoning: proving a safety check dead often needs facts from four
independent LLVM analyses \emph{simultaneously} --- a conjunctive entailment
problem suited to a solver, not to any single dataflow pass.


We instantiate
super-analysis in \tool{}, an LLVM pass that proves sanitizer traps and
native-language safety checks unreachable and removes them.
One mechanism serves four frontend languages and yields speedups in each, including a $3.91\times$ speedup for a GEMM implemented in Julia.
A further solver-based optimization, \emph{guard synthesis}, handles
checks that are not dead for every input: the solver synthesizes a cheap invariant safety guard under which a fast check-free loop is cloned,
speeding up a Swift Base64 encoder by $1.44\times$ and a Julia LZ77
compressor by $4.01\times$. Compared with Souper, the synthesizing
superoptimizer for LLVM, \tool{} removes more safety checks in significantly less time, making it viable as an online compiler pass.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Optimizing compilers decide how fast software runs. Every program in a
compiled language, from operating systems to an artificial intelligence training kernel, passes
through compiler optimization at an intermediate representation (IR) level. A compiler infrastructure such as
LLVM~\cite{lattner2004llvm} serves many input languages at once, including C and C++,
Swift, Rust, and Julia, all compiled to its IR and aggressively optimized through it. Almost all software nowadays goes through such optimization pipelines, so the effectiveness of optimizers heavily influences the performance of the software we use. 

Aggressive compiler
optimization currently falls into two classes. \emph{Conventional optimizers},
such as LLVM's \texttt{-O3} pipeline, apply a fixed sequence of
analyses and rewrites. They are fast: they
make millions of decisions in milliseconds, each justified by a
heuristic or a lightweight analysis that reasons locally and gives up
early. They are perfect for online use in the programmer's develop-and-test loop. \emph{Superoptimizers}~\cite{massalin1987superoptimizer,
schkufza2013stoke, sasnauskas2017souper} search for faster replacement
code and use a solver to prove each candidate correct. They find more optimal code as they extensively search the space of candidate programs, and the solver acts as an equivalence checking oracle.

Each class pays for its strength, and has critical weaknesses. Conventional optimizers are fast, but they miss
optimizations whose safety needs deeper reasoning. Their lightweight conventional heuristics give up and are unable to provide sufficient analysis for many cases~\cite{taneja2020knownbits}. Superoptimizers are more aggressive in optimization as they find
code heuristics cannot, but their search is expensive and coupled with the solver's expensive check, as each candidate code found should be verified correct. Because of this, superoptimizers take up to hours to run, so they are confined to small code windows and run offline,
outside the everyday development and testing loops.

This paper argues for an unexplored middle ground we call \emph{super-analysis}
(Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}): use
the expensive solver-based analysis for \emph{analysis only}, and leave transformation
to the conventional machinery that already exists. No program is synthesized and
no search over the infinite space of programs is performed. The solver answers exactly one kind of question
--- \emph{is this program point reachable under this context?} --- and a
standard, trivially correct transformation (deleting an unreachable branch)
consumes the answer.
The term has been used before for analyses that solve several dataflow problems simultaneously~\cite{wegman1991constant, click1995combining, lerner2002composing}; these combine analyses inside one lattice-based fixpoint, a cheap case of super-analysis. We generalize the notion: a decision procedure combines facts from any analysis, at a cost that places super-analysis between conventional optimization and superoptimization (\S\ref{sec:related}).
We use an SMT solver as an analysis oracle and feed it with the encoded formula $\mathit{Context} \wedge \mathit{Query}$ and ask if it is satisfiable. Soundness is preserved by construction: an
\textsc{unsat} verdict is a proof, a \textsc{sat} or \textsc{unknown}
verdict changes nothing.


SMT-based compiler analysis is more precise than native lightweight compiler analysis. Our central empirical finding is that the missed opportunities require complex reasoning  over multiple domains and from different fact sources to prove safe. Proving
one bounds check dead in a real SHA-256 kernel
(Figure~\ref{fig:simple-example}) requires the return-value
range of one function, a scalar-evolution loop bound, a value-range fact
on a third value, and two dominating branch conditions from the program's prefix ---
\emph{simultaneously}. Each fact lives in a different LLVM analysis; no
single pass computes their conjunction, and the phase-ordering problem
ensures no fixed pipeline ever will. Deciding satisfiability of a
complex conjunction is, in principle, an entailment
problem --- exactly what an SMT solver decides and exactly what a
heuristic cannot. We inspect the unsat core of every proof, so this
claim is not an intuition but a measurement: the cores name the
contributing analyses, and the cores we inspected draw on several sources at once.


We instantiate super-analysis in \tool{}\footnote{On-Demand SMT System.},
an LLVM module pass targeting one of the most consequential classes of
provably-dead code in modern software: \emph{safety checks}. Sanitizers
such as UBSan~\cite{ubsanDocs} and the native checks of memory-safe
languages (Swift bounds and overflow checks, Rust panics, Julia bounds
errors) compile to trap-guarded branches
(Figure~\ref{fig:simple-example}) whose hot-path cost is the
standing tax of memory safety --- we measure it at up to $1.075\times$ on
the zstd library under signed-overflow checks and up to $4.17\times$ on a native-language kernel (Julia GEMM).
Today the only recourse to avoid this performance tax is to turn checks \emph{off}
wholesale (\texttt{-Ounchecked}, \texttt{--check-bounds=no}), trading away
the safety guarantee entirely. \tool{} offers the sound alternative:
delete exactly the checks that are provably redundant with the guards,
contracts, and invariants already present in the program, and keep every
check that is not.
\tool{} is able to recover up to $3.91\times$ of performance by removing redundant safety checks and enabling a cascade of further optimizations.

Deleting checks that are already dead is not \tool{}'s only
optimization. Many hot checks are not dead for every input: they are
dead only for the well-formed inputs a program actually receives, such
as arrays of the expected length. For these, \tool{} performs
\emph{guard synthesis}. The solver synthesizes a guard --- a cheap
condition over values that are invariant inside the loop --- under
which every check in the loop is provably dead. The loop is then
specialized: the guard is checked once, outside the loop, and when it
holds a copy of the loop runs with all its checks removed; otherwise
the original loop runs unchanged. Because real inputs take the
specialized copy, the hot loop becomes the fast one. Guard synthesis
stays within the paradigm: the solver synthesizes a condition, not a
program, and the transformation is an ordinary loop clone. On x86 it
speeds up a Swift Base64 encoder by $1.44\times$ and a Julia LZ77
compressor by $4.01\times$.

Super-analysis is a general compilation paradigm rather than a single
optimization, and it carries one open problem we deliberately defer:
\emph{when is expensive analysis worth invoking?} A solver consulted
indiscriminately at every program point would be ruinous, and deciding
where precision repays its cost demands a cost model no compiler has.
Safety-check elimination is the ideal first instantiation precisely
because it answers that question for free. The work list is given, and the traps are expected to be unreachable (therefore \textsc{unsat}) for safe programs. Based on our experiments, removing a safety check is either a win or has a marginal effect in most cases --- it simplifies the program control-flow, allowing other optimizations to be performed by subsequent passes. This
application therefore isolates the paradigm's contribution --- precision
of analysis --- from the question it defers: when to use it.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Super-analysis} (\S\ref{sec:superanalysis}): an aggressive compilation paradigm
  positioned between conventional heuristic-based optimization and superoptimization in terms of compiler latency and precision. The general recipe for this paradigm is to use solvers as an analysis oracle, while applying cheap conventional rules-based transformation for optimization. We identify heap reasoning as
  a crucial analysis problem, formalize it, and show that
  super-analysis can more effectively reason over heap and memory safety
  of certain transformations.
\item \textbf{Making super-analysis work} (\S\ref{sec:making}): a set of
  techniques inside the paradigm that make it effective. We encode the
  compiler's lightweight native analyses as facts that constrain the
  solver's search, and we
  introduce guard synthesis and loop specialization, which synthesize a
  runtime guard under which a loop's checks are dead and clone the loop
  behind it.
\item \textbf{\tool{}} (\S\ref{sec:system}): an LLVM pass and online optimizer
  as an instantiation of super-analysis. \tool{} eliminates safety checks and unlocks further optimizations. It supports C/C++, Swift, Rust, and Julia as frontends and can enable hot-path transformations and speedups.
\item \textbf{Robust Performance Improvement} (\S\ref{sec:results}):
  our systematic evaluation of \tool{} on an x86 server shows removing hot-loop checks has a cascading effect, unlocking further transformations (unrolling, vectorization, etc.) that enable up to $4.01\times$ speedups for widely used kernels and libraries. \tool{} proofs are generally cheap, which makes it usable as an online pass in the development loop. Multi-threading and per-query budgets make \tool{}'s analysis up to $2.0\times$ faster. A further comparison with Souper (a synthesizing superoptimizer) shows that Thorough removes significantly more checks in less time.
\end{itemize}

\section{Paradigms of Aggressive Compiler Optimization}
\label{sec:related}
Every optimizing pass consists of an analysis that licenses a rewrite and a
transformation that performs it, and each of the two can be cheap or
expensive. By taking the expensive or cheap route for each of the two components we get to the different optimization families in Figure~\ref{fig:quadrants}, which include conventional optimization and superoptimization. We introduce two paradigms that are widely underexplored, super-transformation, of which equality saturation is a strict form, and super-analysis which is the main topic of this paper. To the best of our knowledge we have for the first time systematically described the landscape of optimization paradigms, how they relate and how they differ.

\paragraph{Conventional optimization.} Conventional compiler optimization passes (like in LLVM) consist of analysis modules and transformation rules that consume the analysis to produce more efficient code. Both components are lightweight and fast, sacrificing precision to save compile time and provide rapid feedback. All the other families of optimizers deviate from this simple fast compilation methodology to perform more aggressive transformations.

\paragraph{Superoptimization.} A superoptimizer tries to search a large space of programs for a faster or shorter alternative to the initial code.
Massalin's original superoptimizer~\cite{massalin1987superoptimizer}
enumerated every instruction sequence up to a length bound and kept the
shortest one that matched the target on test inputs. Two lines of work
made the idea provably sound and practical. STOKE~\cite{schkufza2013stoke} replaces
enumeration with stochastic search: a random walk
mutates x86-64 kernels, scores each candidate by test-case correctness
and estimated latency, and hands the survivors to an SMT solver for a
formal equivalence proof, at costs of hours per loop-free kernel.
Souper~\cite{sasnauskas2017souper} works at the LLVM IR level: it
synthesizes replacement code for bounded peephole windows and verifies each
candidate with an SMT solver. In every sound superoptimizer the search proposes code and the solver
verifies it, so the solver is invoked \emph{per candidate}. 

Conventional optimization and superoptimization have been widely explored in the literature, while the asymmetric corners of Figure~\ref{fig:quadrants} are underexplored. We call the paradigm of cheap analysis and expensive transformation super-transformation, which is an exhaustive search over candidate transformations, coupled with cheap analysis. The corner that this paper extensively explores and instantiates is super-analysis: expensive (solver-based) analysis that enables cheap conventional transformations. We can state that superoptimization is a combination of super-transformation (synthesis as the most aggressive exhaustive search for alternative code) and super-analysis (solver-based equivalence checking in sound superoptimizers), making it super expensive.


\paragraph{Equality Saturation.}
Equality
saturation~\cite{tate2009equality} is a disciplined subset of super-transformation: rather than synthesizing candidates, it saturates an
e-graph with the combinations of a fixed rewrite-rule set and extracts
the cheapest result. This aggressive search ranges over combinations of rewrite rules rather than over
arbitrary programs, so no candidate needs an equivalence proof as the applied rules are sound and preserve equivalence.

\paragraph{Super-analysis.} This paper introduces this fourth route. A
super-analyzer spends the solver on \emph{analysis} alone: the solver
decides whether a rewrite is licensed --- here, whether a safety check
can ever fire --- and the compiler's existing, conventional
transformations perform the rewrite. No replacement code is searched
for, synthesized, or verified. \S\ref{sec:superanalysis} develops the
paradigm. The combined analyses that Lerner et al.~\cite{lerner2002composing} call super-analyses fit the same paradigm: they make analysis more expensive and combine different fact sources to license conventional transformations; ours is one of its most aggressive forms, as an SMT solver decides the conjunction of facts from any analysis rather than a product-lattice fixpoint.

Super-analysis shares the verification engine with superoptimization, though the difference in philosophy and architecture makes them radically different in terms of effectiveness and \emph{scaling}. Superoptimizers spend an expensive SMT equivalence checking query per synthesized candidate, making them very slow. A super-analyzing compiler uses the expensive analysis more strategically, consulting the solver whenever its conventional analysis fails and the solver latency is worth the potential unlocked optimization. This makes super-analysis generally faster, something we show in \S\ref{sec:results-souper}. The unscalable nature of synthesis also limits it to small window peephole optimizations, whereas a super-analyzer can reason more globally. For example it can derive and consume post-conditions of complex loops and functions which is beyond the scope of peephole optimizations.

\section{Super-Analysis}
\label{sec:superanalysis}

In this section we describe a general recipe for building a compiler super-analyzer.

\subsection{Encoding program context}
\label{sec:encoding}
A super-analyzer decides a property of a single \emph{program point} ---
here, whether a trap is reachable. The property is decided by
the \emph{context} that leads to the point.
The core machinery of the paradigm is therefore a translation: render
the context of a program point as a logical formula, and ask the solver
whether the property is consistent with it.

The translation proceeds \emph{backwards} from the point of interest.
In this paper we are concerned with safety checks and trap conditions, so starting at the trap condition, it walks the chain of definitions that
feed it and the branch conditions that dominate it, and renders each
instruction as an exact equation over bit-vectors --- arithmetic,
comparisons, shifts, overflow intrinsics, and the control flow
connecting them. The resulting formula is satisfiable exactly by the
machine states that can reach the point, so reachability becomes one
satisfiability query: $\mathit{Context} \wedge \mathit{TrapCond}$.

A backward encoding cannot descend forever. It stops at
\emph{boundaries} --- loop-carried values, function calls, loads:
boxes whose interior it does not open. What crosses a boundary is a
\emph{post-condition}: a fact true of the box's result in every
execution. The weakest post-condition is no constraint at all --- the
value is a free variable --- and this is the soundness floor: anything
not understood is over-approximated, so an \textsc{unsat} verdict holds
for every concrete behavior of the unencoded parts. From that floor,
post-condition derivation is a precision dial that can in principle be
turned arbitrarily far: a range, an algebraic relation among boundary
values, a loop invariant, a full summary. 

\subsection{Heap-Invariant Super-Analysis}
\label{sec:frame}

Encoding the context alone does not prove every property an optimizer
needs, because many of these properties concern the state of the heap:
optimizations depend on the values that loads return and on the
pointers they read through. A dynamic array's length, for example, is
stored as metadata in memory (in the array's header), so the length an
index is checked against is a load, and reasoning about it requires a
model of the heap. A compiler's scalar analyses do not provide one.
Each describes the values that one SSA register may hold: in LLVM,
value ranges (LazyValueInfo and range metadata), known bits, and the
trip counts and induction ranges of scalar evolution. They can
constrain the value of a single load, but they cannot relate two loads
to each other.

Figure~\ref{fig:frame-example} illustrates the need for heap-aware
reasoning. The example is a safety check in the inner loop of a matrix
multiply. Before the loop, a dimension guard loads \texttt{A}'s length
into $n_1$ and checks that it equals the loop bound $m$. Inside the
loop, the bounds check loads the length again, into $n_2$, and the loop
also stores into \texttt{C}. Since $i < m = n_1$, the check is dead if
$n_2 = n_1$, and scalar analyses cannot establish that, for two
reasons. First, a load is opaque to them: its result is whatever memory
holds, so without metadata $n_2$ may be any 64-bit value. Second,
$n_2 = n_1$ is a relation between two values, while a range or a set of
known bits describes one value at a time. The equality holds only if
the store between the two reads never overwrites \texttt{A}'s length,
which is a question about memory, answered in LLVM by alias analysis and
MemorySSA. Here they do not answer it: the reload is a copy made by the
vectorizer, without the aliasing metadata that would let alias analysis
rule out the store. The check survives \texttt{-O3}, and a solver given
only scalar facts treats $n_2$ as a free variable and finds the trap
reachable. The checks limit how much the loop is vectorized and
unrolled: in \texttt{generic\_matmatmul!}, the matrix multiply of
Julia's standard library, they cost $4.17\times$ on x86
(\S\ref{sec:frame-results}).

Heap-invariant super-analysis supplies the missing fact. A
super-analyzer can reason via axiomatic rules and encode the facts they
yield: here, that two loads through the same pointer return the same
value if no store in between overwrites the location --- an instance of
the frame rule of separation logic~\cite{reynolds2002separation}.
Super-analysis adds precision where conventional scalar analyses fail
(e.g., LLVM's known bits), and its special case, heap-invariant
super-analysis, takes over where conventional memory analysis fails
(e.g., LLVM's MemorySSA), reusing that analysis's answers to derive new
facts. A heap fact alone is rarely enough: the solver conjoins it with
the scalar facts --- for GEMM, the load equalities together with
trip-count, known-bits, and definitional facts --- and it is the
combination that proves the check dead. The encoding machinery that
handles GEMM (\S\ref{sec:frame-machinery}) also added proofs on other
benchmarks, such as the Swift and Julia SHA-256 implementations and
CryptoSwift.

\subsection{Use Case: Safety Checks}
\label{sec:target}
We instantiate super-analysis on the \emph{safety checks} that compilers insert into
programs. A safety check guards an operation whose result would
otherwise be undefined or unsafe --- an array access out of bounds, a
signed integer overflow --- with a test and a branch to a block that
stops the program if the test fails. Every front end we support inserts
them. In C and C++, UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer~\cite{ubsanDocs} adds a
check for each kind of undefined behavior it is asked to catch; in trap
mode each one ends in a call to \texttt{llvm.ubsantrap}. Safer languages inject safety checks as part of their semantics. Swift checks
every array access and every arithmetic overflow by default, in
optimized builds too, and stops with \texttt{llvm.trap}~\cite{swiftBook}.
Rust checks every slice and array index, and every arithmetic operation
when overflow checks are enabled, and stops with a call to a panic
function~\cite{rustReference}. Julia checks every array access and stops
by throwing a bounds error~\cite{juliaBoundsCheck}. After lowering to
LLVM IR all of them have one shape --- a conditional branch to a block
that calls a function that never returns --- and that shape is what
\tool{} looks for (\S\ref{sec:pipeline}).


The checks make programs safe, and the safety has a price. In Julia's
standard-library matrix multiply, the check Julia inserts for the
access \texttt{A[i,l]} runs on every iteration of the innermost loop,
the hottest loop of the program, and as a
side exit from that loop it keeps LLVM from vectorizing and unrolling
the loop as fully as the check-free version: with the checks turned off
this kernel runs $4.17\times$ faster on x86. Today the only way to recover that performance
is to turn checks off wholesale (\texttt{@inbounds},
\texttt{--check-bounds=no}, \texttt{-Ounchecked}), giving up the guarantee
for the whole loop or program. \tool{} instead removes only the checks it
proves can never fail, so the program stays exactly as safe. Because
Swift and Rust keep their checks in optimized builds, \tool{} applies
to the large body of existing Swift and Rust code without any source
change --- two of our Swift benchmarks, crc32 and adler32, are taken
unmodified from existing code bases (\S\ref{sec:results-perf}) --- for
example as a step in release builds.

\section{Making Super-Analysis Work}
\label{sec:making}
\S\ref{sec:superanalysis} gave the general framework and recipe for super-analysis; this section collects the techniques that make
it practical and useful. Like
\S\ref{sec:superanalysis}, it is a general recipe, independent of
LLVM and applicable to any compiler. Encoding a program point's context may not provide enough constraints for a useful proof, therefore we import the lightweight facts a
conventional compiler analysis already computes, to further constrain the solver's search
(\S\ref{sec:conjunction}). Also, some checks
cannot be proven dead for every execution, no matter how precise the analysis is. We do not give up in these cases: we synthesize an invariant guard under
which checks are dead and clone the loop behind it (\S\ref{sec:guards}).

\subsection{Encoding Lightweight Heuristics}
\label{sec:conjunction}
Encoding the context alone does not produce every useful proof. The
encoding stops at boundaries, and a boundary value is free: the solver
may pick any value for it, including values no execution can produce.
Yet the compiler has usually learned something about that value
already. Its lightweight, fast native analyses --- value ranges, known
bits, loop trip counts --- run in milliseconds, and each describes a
slice of the truth. Importing their results as facts about the boundary
values constrains the solver's search space to executions that can
really happen. No single one of these facts is usually enough, though:
a proof needs several of them at once, and composing them is exactly
where a solver becomes necessary.


Consider again the simplified message-schedule loop of the Swift SHA-256
kernel of the introduction (Figure~\ref{fig:simple-example}), whose inner
16 iterations index a 64-element working array. Swift's bounds checks on
these accesses survive both \texttt{swiftc -O} and a subsequent full LLVM
O3. \tool{} proves them dead, and the extracted unsat cores show why no
single heuristic could. Each proof's minimal core has the shape:
\[
\textsf{Spec} \wedge \textsf{RM} \wedge \textsf{SCEV} \wedge \textsf{LVI}
\wedge \textsf{SCEV}' \wedge G_0 \wedge G_k \;\vdash\; \bot
\]
--- a return-value range (\textsf{RM}), two scalar-evolution facts
(\textsf{SCEV}), a lattice value range (\textsf{LVI}), and two dominating
guard conditions from the context ($G_0$, $G_k$), jointly inconsistent with the check
firing (\textsf{Spec}). Four independent analyses and path conditions must be intersected;
each in isolation is insufficient (removing any core member returns
\textsc{sat}). LLVM's analyses do not compose this way: each consumes the
IR and produces facts in its own domain, and no pass computes the
satisfiability of their conjunction --- nor could a fixed pipeline, since
which facts matter is different at every check site. Deciding such
conjunctions is the definitional competence of an SMT
solver~\cite{demoura2008z3}, making super-analysis necessary rather than merely convenient. Combining analyses is the classic answer to this problem~\cite{click1995combining, lerner2002composing}; those combinations stay within one product lattice, whereas the solver decides the conjunction of facts from different domains directly.

\subsection{Guard Synthesis and Loop Cloning}
\label{sec:guards}
Sometimes a check cannot be proved dead for every execution, because it
is not dead for every execution. Consider the Julia LZ77 compressor in our benchmarks. Its inner loop reads
\texttt{data[i+len]} only while \texttt{i+len <= n}, where $n$ is the
length of \texttt{data}, a value known only at run time. In
unbounded integer arithmetic this test makes the bounds check on
\texttt{data[i+len]} redundant. In 64-bit arithmetic it does not: if \texttt{n} is close to $2^{63}-1$, \texttt{i+len} can exceed $2^{63}-1$, the largest 64-bit integer. In this case Julia
integer arithmetic silently overflows, and the solver answers with a satisfying assignment in which \texttt{i+len} overflows to $-2^{63}$, passing the programmer's safety guard \texttt{i+len <= n} but triggering an out-of-bounds index (negative index) at \texttt{data[i+len]}. This vulnerability truly exists, and the safety check must remain for this array access.

Yet no real call looks like these models: an array of nearly $2^{63}$
bytes, about 9 exabytes, exists on no machine, and for every array that
does, every bounds check in the loop is dead. The property is hard to prove for the loop, but easy to prove
for a \emph{constrained} version of it --- and the constrained version is
the frequent one, often the only one that ever runs.

Guard synthesis exploits this. Instead of asking whether the check is
dead, we ask \emph{under what condition} it is dead: an invariant condition over
values that do not change inside the loop, cheap enough to check once
before the loop starts. Given such a guard, the loop is cloned
(Figure~\ref{fig:guard}). The fast copy has its checks removed; the
original, with every check intact, runs whenever the guard fails. For
\texttt{lz77\_scan}, guided by those models, the solver synthesizes
\[
0 \le n \le 2^{62},
\]
which holds for every array a real machine can allocate, so the fast
copy is the one that always executes.

The transformation itself is simple; the hard part is finding the right guard. It must
be a smart, useful guard: weak enough to hold on the executions we care
about, and loop-invariant and cheap, so that checking it at run time costs nothing
measurable. And it must be sufficient: under the guard, the fast copy
must be provably safe to run. Both are questions about the conjunction
of the guard, the context, and the trap condition, so, again, they
necessitate a solver. The solver proposes the guard, verifies that the
trap is unreachable under it, and verifies that the guard does not
contradict the context as a contradictory guard would make the fast
copy dead code.

The idea resembles abductive inference~\cite{dillig2012abductive,
dillig2013abductive}. Given known facts $\Gamma$ and a goal $\varphi$,
abduction seeks a hypothesis $\psi$ that is \emph{sufficient} and
\emph{consistent}:
\[
\Gamma \wedge \psi \models \varphi
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\Gamma \wedge \psi \not\models \bot .
\]
Guard synthesis instantiates this directly. $\Gamma$ is the encoded
context of the check, with its dominating guards and imported facts; the
goal is that the trap is unreachable, $\varphi = \neg\,\mathit{TrapCond}$; and
the hypothesis is the guard $H$, restricted to values that do not change
inside the loop. Since $\Gamma \wedge H \models \neg\,\mathit{TrapCond}$
holds exactly when $\Gamma \wedge H \wedge \mathit{TrapCond}$ has no model,
each condition becomes one solver query:
\[
\underbrace{\Gamma \wedge H \wedge \mathit{TrapCond}\ \text{is \textsc{unsat}}}_{\text{sufficient: the fast copy is safe}}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\underbrace{\Gamma \wedge H\ \text{is \textsc{sat}}}_{\text{consistent: the guard is not vacuous}} .
\]
A guard that failed the second query would make the trap unreachable
only because the fast copy itself could never run. Among the guards that
pass both, we prefer the weakest, so that it holds on as many real
inputs as possible.

\section{\tool{}: Design and Implementation}
\label{sec:system}
\tool{} is an LLVM module pass of roughly five thousand lines that removes safety checks and enables further optimizations.
This section describes the pipeline the pass lives in
(\S\ref{sec:pipeline}), the encoding machinery at its core
(\S\ref{sec:encoding-impl}), inductive loop encoding
(\S\ref{sec:app-machinery}), the heap-invariance extension
(\S\ref{sec:frame-machinery}), guard synthesis and loop cloning
(\S\ref{sec:guards-impl}), and how proofs are audited before they are
believed (\S\ref{sec:audit}).

\subsection{Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}
\tool{} runs sandwiched between two full \texttt{-O3} pipelines. The
first produces the optimized IR with inserted checks it could not prove dead; that is the analysis
input. The second, after \tool{}'s check elimination, re-optimizes the simplified
control flow and performs the transformations the deleted checks were
blocking --- unrolling, vectorization (\S\ref{sec:results-mech}). 

Internally the pass is three stages. \emph{Serial discovery} scans the
module for trap sites: calls to
\texttt{llvm.ubsantrap}/\texttt{llvm.trap}. Each incoming edge of a
trap block becomes one independent \emph{job}
(\S\ref{sec:encoding-impl}).
\emph{Parallel solve}: workers claim jobs in discovery order, each
owning a private Z3 context; the IR is read-only throughout. The only
shared mutable state is LLVM's own analyses, whose internal caches make
concurrent queries both a data race and a determinism hazard --- so
every analysis query passes through a ticket turnstile keyed on
discovery index, serializing them in one canonical order. That single
mechanism buys thread safety \emph{and} reproducibility: verdicts,
output IR, and logs are identical for \texttt{threads=1} and
\texttt{threads=N}, unless a query finishes close to its time budget, where
CPU contention can decide whether it completes (at 10\,s per query, three
Julia SHA-256 proofs and four of CryptoSwift's 377; \S\ref{sec:audit}). \emph{Serial
transform}: the main thread folds each proven branch to a constant in
discovery order --- the only IR mutation in the pass --- and standard
\texttt{simplifycfg}/\texttt{adce} reclaim the dead blocks. Workers
fence all exceptions and degrade to keeping the trap: every error path
in the system converges on declining to optimize.

An encoding thread starts from a trap \emph{edge}, which is a boolean condition $c$ that takes the control-flow from a predecessor basic block $P$ to a trap block. Frontends merge error blocks, so a trap block routinely has many incoming
edges. Each qualifying edge is an independent obligation: the trap fires via this edge
iff control reaches $P$ and $c$ holds. Removing the exit branch from $P$ via $c$ to a shared trap block is sound
and profitable, even if the trap remains reachable from other blocks.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Encoding and deciding one trap edge $(P, c)$}
\label{alg:encode}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $G \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{dominating context}
\ForAll{dominators $D$ of $P$ ending in a cond.\ branch}
  \If{one outgoing edge of $D$ dominates $P$}
    $G \gets G \cup \{$that edge's condition$\}$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $S \gets$ every instruction that $c$ and $G$ depend on, collected
  backwards through operands \Comment{the slice}
\Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent} (including phis that merge branches; stopping at loads, calls,
  and loop phis)
\ForAll{$I \in S$ in reverse post-order}
  \State assert $\den{I}$ per Table~\ref{tab:encoding}
  \Comment{boundaries: fresh variables}
\EndFor
\State assert every $g \in G$ (tracked)
\State \Return $\mathrm{check\mbox{-}sat}(c)$
\Comment{\textsc{unsat}: fold edge; \textsc{sat}/\textsc{unknown}: keep}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Encoding a job proceeds backwards from $c$ in three steps
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:encode}).
\emph{(1)~Guards.} Walk the dominator tree upward from $P$: for every
dominator ending in a conditional branch with one outgoing edge that
dominates $P$, every execution reaching $P$ took that edge, so the
edge's condition is asserted as context --- SSA immutability
guarantees its operands still denote the same values at the trap.
\emph{(2)~Slice.} A worklist walks operands backwards from $c$ and
from every guard. It stops at the \emph{boundaries} of
\S\ref{sec:encoding}: loads, address computations, calls we do not
encode, and loop-header phis, all of which become free variables. A
phi at an ordinary merge point is entered instead: its incoming values
and the branch conditions of its merge region join the slice. Every
cycle of data dependences in SSA runs through a loop-header phi, where
the walk stops, so the walk follows an acyclic graph and visits each value
once: it always terminates, and the slice is at most the size of the
function.
\emph{(3)~Translation.} The slice is encoded in reverse post-order by
the rules of Table~\ref{tab:encoding}: one exact bit-vector equation
per instruction, at the instruction's own width. A merge-point phi becomes
an if-then-else chain gated by a reachability formula that is built once per block:
\[
\mathit{Reach}(B) \;=\; \textstyle\bigvee_{P \in \mathrm{preds}(B)}
\mathit{Reach}(P) \wedge \mathit{Edge}(P{\to}B),
\]
The formula is an $O(\mathit{edges})$ circuit whose shared prefixes are stored once
(Z3 terms are hash-consed), leaving path enumeration to the SAT
engine rather than to an exponential DNF.

We encode LLVM's semantics of poison and
\texttt{freeze}~\cite{lee2017taming}, following
Alive2~\cite{lopes2021alive2}, which capture how undefined behavior
spreads through a program. Loaded values are assumed not to be poison: memory holds only values the
program stored, and poison can be stored only after undefined behavior that
the source languages either forbid in safe code (Rust, Swift) or check (our
UBSan specifications). The \texttt{poison-loads} knob drops this assumption.

The query is then $\mathit{Guards} \wedge \mathit{Definitions} \wedge
c$: satisfiable exactly by the machine states, consistent with
everything encoded, in which this edge reaches the trap.
\textsc{unsat} proves the edge dead in every execution;
\textsc{sat} and \textsc{unknown} (timeout) keep the check.

\subsection{Encoding LLVM's analyses}
\label{sec:system-facts}
The encoding of \S\ref{sec:encoding-impl} is the \emph{light} tier: it encodes the context alone and gives every boundary --- a load, a call, a loop-header phi --- an unconstrained variable. This over-approximates the values the boundaries can take, and the solver often answers \textsc{sat} with a model no execution can produce. The \emph{heavy} tier strengthens the
boundaries with post-conditions bought from LLVM's own analyses,
asserted as labeled constraints on the free variables:
\texttt{!range} metadata and range attributes (RM), KnownBits (KB),
LazyValueInfo ranges (LVI), scalar-evolution constant ranges (SCEV),
and a symbolic trip-count translation (SCEVSYM) that renders loop
bounds --- including symbolic starts and an exact unsigned-division
translation --- as constraints on loop-header phis. The values these
trip-count facts mention, such as the vectorizer's resume indices, are
themselves encoded by the rules of Table~\ref{tab:encoding} rather than
left free, and the bounds are stated in a form that is safe under
wrap-around (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-defs}). In debug mode
(\S\ref{sec:audit}) every fact is tracked under its label, so the unsat
core of each proof names the fact sources and mechanisms that contributed
to it; these are the labels in the cores of \S\ref{sec:conjunction} and
\S\ref{sec:frame-results}. 

Fast and Thorough (\S\ref{sec:results-cost}) combine these knobs. Besides these, the user sets the two latency dials, \texttt{timeout=} and \texttt{threads=} (\S\ref{sec:results-cost}); Appendix~\ref{sec:app-knobs} lists every knob.

\subsection{Encoding loops inductively}
\label{sec:app-machinery}
Hot loops hold the checks that cost the most, so we do not abandon such prime targets. We do not just rely
on LLVM's analyses either: we also encode constraints derived from the loop
itself. A loop-header phi merges a loop variable's value on entry with the
value its back edge carries from the previous iteration. Loop-header
phis are boundaries (\S\ref{sec:encoding}), so a check
whose safety depends on how a loop's values evolve is out of reach of a
single query unless a fact source bounds those values. Scalar evolution
often does (SCEVSYM), but not for loops that advance by a variable or
non-unit stride, or exit in several places. It is also not the most precise loop post-condition or invariant deriver.  \tool{}'s \texttt{ind} knob
proves such checks by one-step induction over the loop, with no trip
count and no invariant synthesis. Each loop-header phi has the form
\[
\phi = \mathtt{phi}\ [\phi_{\mathit{init}},\ \mathit{preheader}],\ [\phi_{\mathit{ind}},\ \mathit{BackEdge}],
\]
where $\phi_{\mathit{init}}$ is the value on entry to the loop and
$\phi_{\mathit{ind}}$ the value the back edge carries, computed in the
loop body. Let $\vec\phi_k$ be the vector of values of the trap's loop-header phis
in iteration $k$, and $\Gamma_k$ and $\mathit{TrapCond}_k$ the encoded
context, with its imported facts, and the trap condition in
iteration $k$; iterations $k$ and $k{+}1$ are encoded as two copies of
the loop body over separate variables, which in effect unrolls the loop
once. Two queries are posed:
\begin{align*}
\textsc{Base}:&\quad \Gamma_0 \wedge \vec\phi_0 = \vec\phi_{\mathit{init}} \wedge \mathit{TrapCond}_0\\
\textsc{Step}:&\quad \bigl(\Gamma_k \wedge \neg\,\mathit{TrapCond}_k \wedge \mathit{BackEdge}_k\bigr)
  && \text{iteration $k$: the check did not fire}\\
&\quad\wedge\ \vec\phi_{k+1} = \vec\phi_{\mathit{ind, k}}
  && \text{the back edge}\\
&\quad\wedge\ \bigl(\Gamma_{k+1} \wedge \mathit{TrapCond}_{k+1}\bigr)
  && \text{iteration $k{+}1$: the check fires}
\end{align*}
where $\mathit{BackEdge}_k$ states that iteration $k$ took the back
edge, $\vec\phi_{\mathit{ind, k}}$ is the back-edge value encoded in iteration $k$, and
$\neg\,\mathit{TrapCond}_k$ states that the check did not fire in
iteration $k$. If both are
\textsc{unsat}, the check cannot fire on the first iteration, nor on any
iteration that follows one on which it did not fire; by induction it
never fires. Iteration $k$ is
arbitrary, so the step query leaves $\vec\phi_k$ unconstrained except by
the facts below. \tool{} poses the two queries only when the direct query is
\textsc{sat} or \textsc{unknown} and the trap lies in a loop, first for
the trap's own loop and then for each enclosing one. Importing the facts of iteration $k$ queries scalar evolution, which is
not thread-safe, so the facts are imported through the turnstile of
\S\ref{sec:pipeline} and the two queries run in parallel like any other.

\subsection{Encoding heap invariance}
\label{sec:frame-machinery}
We add a heap and pointer reasoning machinery to our pass. The \texttt{frame} encoding knob supplies the heap fact of \S\ref{sec:frame}:
two loads of the same location return the same value if no store
between them can write that location. Two notions from LLVM decide this.
A store (or call) \emph{clobbers} a load if it may write to the location
the load reads. \emph{MemorySSA} is LLVM's analysis that links each load
to the most recent memory write that may clobber it, using alias
analysis to skip writes that provably touch other locations.

For any two loads of the same pointer $p$, \texttt{n1 = load p} and
\texttt{n2 = load p}, where every path to \texttt{n2} passes through
\texttt{n1}, \tool{} adds the equality
\[
\frac{\mathrm{clobber}(n_2,\ p) \ \preceq\ \mathrm{def}(n_1)}
     {\den{n_1} = \den{n_2}}
\]
Read as an inference rule: $\mathrm{clobber}(n_2, p)$ is the most recent
write before $n_2$ that may clobber $p$, as MemorySSA reports it;
$\mathrm{def}(n_1)$ is the most recent write before $n_1$; and
$a \preceq b$ means that write instruction $a$ is $b$ itself or comes before it. The
premise therefore says that the last write that may change $p$ before
$n_2$ had already happened when $n_1$ read $p$. If the premise
holds, nothing between the two loads can change the location, and the
two loaded values are equal: a new encoded constraint. With no write
between the loads the premise holds trivially; with writes between them,
MemorySSA must show that none of them can write the location. This is exactly
the case of Figure~\ref{fig:frame-example}, where \texttt{n1} is read by
the dimension guard and \texttt{n2} by the check, and the equality
$n_2 = n_1$ is what proves the check dead.

\tool{} adds no alias analysis of
its own: every disjointness verdict is LLVM's, and when MemorySSA cannot
rule a write out, no fact is added. \tool{} only combines LLVM's analysis results with the other constraints, and that suffices for GEMM (Figure~\ref{fig:frame-example}): it recovers $3.91\times$ of the $4.17\times$ the checks cost (\S\ref{sec:frame-results}). A super-analyzer could reason itself about pointers, encoding address arithmetic and aliasing in the
solver, but more precise alias information does not by itself make code
faster: removing LLVM's alias analysis changes the final binaries little,
and only a small fraction of alias queries affects them~\cite{relaxingalias2025}.

\subsection{Implementing Guard Synthesis}
\label{sec:guards-impl}
\tool{} attempts guard synthesis (pass parameters \texttt{mv} and
\texttt{narrow}) only for a trap whose direct query returned
\textsc{sat} or \textsc{unknown}, and only when the trap sits inside a
loop. A trap that is already proved dead never reaches this step, so
guard synthesis can add proofs but never lose one. It proceeds in five
steps (Algorithm~\ref{alg:guards}): generate candidate guards, verify
that the trap is dead under them, shrink them to a small guard, check
the guard for vacuity, and transform the loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Guard synthesis for one trap edge $(P, c)$ inside loop $L$}
\label{alg:guards}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require the direct query $\Gamma \wedge c$ returned \textsc{sat} or
  \textsc{unknown} \Comment{$\Gamma$: context, facts, guards}
\State $C \gets$ candidates from templates T1--T3 over values
  invariant in $L$ \Comment{generation}
\If{$\mathrm{check\mbox{-}sat}(\Gamma \wedge c \wedge \bigwedge C) \neq
  \textsc{unsat}$} \Comment{verification}
  \State \Return \textit{keep the trap}
\EndIf
\State $H \gets C \cap \mathrm{unsat\mbox{-}core}$ of that query \Comment{a sufficient subset}
\ForAll{$h \in H$, upper bounds first, tightest first}
  \Comment{minimization}
  \If{$\mathrm{check\mbox{-}sat}(\Gamma \wedge c \wedge
    \bigwedge (H \setminus \{h\})) = \textsc{unsat}$}
    \State $H \gets H \setminus \{h\}$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\ForAll{upper bounds $v \le_s 2^k$ in $H$} \Comment{loosening}
  \State raise $k$ while $\mathrm{check\mbox{-}sat}(\Gamma \wedge c \wedge \bigwedge H) = \textsc{unsat}$
\EndFor
\If{$\mathrm{check\mbox{-}sat}(\Gamma \wedge \bigwedge H) =
  \textsc{unsat}$} \Comment{vacuity check}
  \State \Return \textit{keep the trap}
\EndIf
\If{an operand of $H$ does not dominate the preheader of $L$}
  \State \Return \textit{keep the trap}
\EndIf
\State clone $L$ into $L_{\mathit{fast}}$; in the preheader, branch on
  $\bigwedge H$ \Comment{transformation}
\Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent} to $L_{\mathit{fast}}$ if it holds,
  to the original $L$ otherwise
\State in $L_{\mathit{fast}}$, fold the trap edge to its non-trap
  successor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Templates.} Every candidate is a comparison over values that
do not change inside the loop, so it can be evaluated once in the loop's
preheader. Three templates generate the candidates.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{T1, length versus index.} When the trap condition is
  $\mathit{idx} \ge_u \mathit{count}$, with $\mathit{count}$
  loop-invariant and $\mathit{idx}$ varying, propose
  $\mathit{count} >_u \mathit{hi}$, where $\mathit{hi}$ is the largest
  value the index can take according to its static unsigned range.
\item \textbf{T2, sane range.} For every loop-invariant integer input
  $v$, propose $0 \le_s v$ and a ladder of upper bounds $v \le_s 2^k$:
  far above realistic sizes, far below overflow.
\item \textbf{T3, symbolic index bound.} When the index is an affine
  function of the loop counters, compute its largest value over the
  whole loop nest from the loops' trip counts, and propose
  $\mathit{count} >_u \max(\mathit{idx})$. For \texttt{matmul!} this
  yields $|a| > n^2 - 1$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Verification.} All candidates are asserted together, each
tracked, on top of the context, the facts, and the trap condition. If
the solver answers \textsc{unsat}, the trap is dead under their
conjunction, and the unsat core names a sufficient subset $H$. If it
answers \textsc{sat} or \textsc{unknown}, no guard the templates can
express suffices, and the trap is kept.

\paragraph{Minimization and loosening.} Cores are not minimal. We drop
the conjuncts of $H$ one at a time, upper bounds first and tightest
first, keeping every drop that stays \textsc{unsat}. We then try to
relax each remaining upper bound, for example from $2^{15}$ to $2^{30}$,
while the query stays \textsc{unsat}. The result is a guard that holds
on as many real inputs as the proof allows.

\paragraph{Vacuity.} A guard that contradicts the context makes the trap
trivially unreachable, and the fast copy dead code. We therefore check
that $\Gamma \wedge H$ is satisfiable, and refuse the guard otherwise.

\paragraph{Transformation.} Every operand of $H$ must dominate the
loop's preheader, so that $H$ can be evaluated there. The loop is
cloned; a branch on $H$ in the preheader selects the fast copy or the
original; in the fast copy, the proven trap branches are folded. Nested
loops are handled innermost first, each guard is hoisted to the
outermost loop in which it is invariant, and later clones inherit the
folds already made in earlier ones. When a verification query contains
64-bit multiplications that the solver struggles with, it is re-encoded
at 32 bits with explicit overflow flags; this is sound only under $H$,
which bounds the inputs, so it is used only to certify guards.

\subsection{Debug Mode}
\label{sec:audit}
We take multiple measures to ensure the absence of data races,
parallelism bugs, and encoding bugs. These checks run in a debug mode as each one would impose a performance tax on the pass. First,
verdicts, output IR, and logs must be identical for \texttt{threads=1}
and \texttt{threads=N} up to the time-budget exception of \S\ref{sec:pipeline}, which we verify by \texttt{diff}.

To make sure our tool is sound, no
\textsc{unsat} is accepted at face value and is inspected. We perform a \texttt{vacuity} test, meaning that we remove the trap condition from the \texttt{UNSAT} proof and query the solver again with all the other constraints. If
the context alone is already unsatisfiable --- an encoder bug, or a
genuinely contradictory guard chain --- the elimination is
\emph{refused} and reported. The audit is deliberately
self-distrusting: on zstd it surrendered 61 likely-sound eliminations
whose guard chains are jointly infeasible for the actual constants,
because the tool cannot distinguish that situation from its own bugs;
vacuous counts are reported in every static audit and are zero in the vast majority of cases, so a vacuous proof can be considered a warning. The timed builds run without this audit to avoid an extra SMT query, for speed; guard
synthesis checks its own guards for vacuity in every build
(\S\ref{sec:guards-impl}).

Independently, every encoded solver constraint is tracked, so each
\textsc{unsat} yields a minimal core naming exactly the guards and
facts that proved it; we inspect the cores of every nontrivial proof
family by hand before counting it.

We collected 17 benchmarks, 6 of them from existing libraries and repositories or extracted from them, and the rest are our implementations of memory- and compute-heavy everyday kernels. Table~\ref{tab:static} lists the benchmarks, their sources, and the percentage of their checks that \tool{} removes.

\subsection{RQ2: What is the source of speedup?}
\label{sec:results-mech}
In this section we explain how proofs unlock massive speedups, and in our main ablation study we explore which components of our guard synthesis procedure mainly contribute to performance improvement. 

A removed check pays exactly when it was blocking a further
transformation. A check is a side exit; inside a hot loop it prevents
unrolling and vectorization, and once it is gone the second
\texttt{-O3} performs them, so a few proofs can cascade into a large
speedup. SHA-256 is the typical case: seven proofs in the
message-schedule loop let it unroll, and the kernel runs $1.107\times$
faster on x86. Where the proofs land
matters more than how many there are: 207 cold proofs leave zlib flat
($1.011\times$), while 16 on zstd's compression path buy $1.065\times$
of a $1.075\times$ ceiling.

\paragraph{Heap invariance.}
\label{sec:frame-results}
Heap invariance unlocks stdlib GEMM, the second-largest speedup in
Table~\ref{tab:speedups}. Its innermost loop, the hottest code of the
kernel, makes four array accesses per iteration: it reads \texttt{A[i,l]},
\texttt{B[l,j]}, and \texttt{C[i,j]}, and writes \texttt{C[i,j]}, and Julia
guards each with a bounds check that reloads the array's length from its
header (Figure~\ref{fig:frame-example}). With the scalar facts alone,
\tool{} proves none of these checks: each reload is a fresh variable. With
the frame facts of \S\ref{sec:frame-machinery}, each reload is equated
with the load of the same length before the loop, where Julia's own
dimension guard compares it with the loop bound, and \tool{} proves 9 of
the kernel's 16 trap edges, among them every check of the read of
\texttt{A} and of the write of \texttt{C}. The proof of the read's check
draws on six fact sources at once
($|\mathrm{SCEVSYM}|\,|\mathrm{KB}|\,|\mathrm{SCEVEQ}|{\times}3\,
|\mathrm{FRAME}|{\times}3$, plus four guards).
These two accesses are the ones that matter. Each check is a side exit
from the innermost loop, and with the read of \texttt{A} and the write of
\texttt{C} unchecked, LLVM aggressively unrolls and vectorizes the loop as it does the
check-free version. Julia's JIT cannot consume the pass output, so we
annotate exactly the two proven accesses \texttt{@inbounds} and leave the
reads of \texttt{B} and \texttt{C} checked: the kernel runs $3.911\times$
faster on x86, of the $4.174\times$ that removing all four checks gives. The performance improvement is robust as \tool{}'s build is
significantly faster at every one of 14 problem shapes, by $1.22$--$4.12\times$
(Figure~\ref{fig:gemm-sweep}, Appendix~\ref{sec:app-full}).

\paragraph{Guard synthesis: which component pays?}
Most of the other large speedups --- Julia lz77 ($4.01\times$), base64
($1.44\times$), Rust matmul ($1.38\times$), Julia matmul, crc32 --- come
from guard synthesis and loop cloning (\S\ref{sec:guards}). We are interested in knowing if our synthesis algorithm makes the difference, or \tool{} verification and proving ability is again buying us the performance. Therefore we isolate the contribution of each component in this experiment.

Cloning a
loop behind a runtime test is not new (\S\ref{sec:relwork}): LLVM's own
inductive range-check elimination (IRCE) splits a loop around its range
checks and versions it behind a runtime range test. Using IRCE we can answer the question: is it our smart guards or is it \tool{}'s proving ability that makes the difference? We
separate the two components with five pipelines that combine the two parts with
LLVM's counterparts:
(i)~\tool{} with guard synthesis: \tool{}'s guard and \tool{}'s proofs;
(ii)~\tool{}'s guard and loop copy, with every check left in place, so
that \texttt{-O3} must remove them: \tool{}'s guard, LLVM's proofs;
(iii)~IRCE followed by \tool{} without guard synthesis: LLVM's guard,
\tool{}'s proofs;
(iv)~IRCE followed by \texttt{-O3}: LLVM's guard and proofs; and
(v)~\texttt{-O3} alone.

\subsection{RQ3: Is \tool{} fast enough for real-world use?}
\label{sec:results-cost}
\paragraph{Compile cost.}
Compile-time latency is the first concern when it comes to SMT-assisted optimization, and it has limited previous work to offline use~\cite{sasnauskas2017souper, schkufza2013stoke, taneja2020knownbits}. A compiler pass must be fast to be widely adopted by programmers as an online pass, and we use three measures to make \tool{} faster: massive parallelism, strict SMT timeouts, and relaxed encoding precision. This makes \tool{}'s cost flexible, and our results show that it is suitable for online use inside the programmer's development loop.

To make \tool{} easy and simple to use, we introduce two tiers it can run in: \emph{Fast} and \emph{Thorough}. Fast uses strict 10\,ms timeouts with 10 threads, and encodes the imported LLVM analyses (\S\ref{sec:system-facts}), heap invariance (\S\ref{sec:frame-machinery}), and guard synthesis (\S\ref{sec:guards-impl}), without inductive encoding. Thorough is the configuration behind the speedup table (Table~\ref{tab:speedups}) and optimizes more aggressively, using a generous 10\,s timeout, 10-thread parallelism, and all the fruitful knobs on: the imported analyses, heap invariance, inductive encoding (\S\ref{sec:app-machinery}), and guard synthesis (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-knobs} lists both configurations).

Table~\ref{tab:compile} (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-full}) gives the \tool{}
stage time of both configurations on every benchmark of
Table~\ref{tab:speedups}; we summarize it here. On the 14 kernels,
\emph{Thorough}, the configuration behind our speedup table, takes a
median of 2.3\,s, and 11 of them compile in under 10\,s; the slowest is
Julia SHA-256, at 128\,s. \emph{Fast} takes a median of 0.53\,s and at
most 2.3\,s, against about 0.1\,s for a conventional \texttt{-O3}. On
the three repositories Thorough takes 147\,s (CryptoSwift), 117\,s
(zstd), and 1624\,s (zlib), and Fast 70\,s, 33\,s, and 539\,s. These are massive repositories, and a developer often cares more about per-module latency as each module is compiled individually in the development loop. The median zlib module takes 5.0\,s
under Thorough and 1.6\,s under Fast, the median zstd module 1.4\,s
and 0.41\,s, and 24 of the 32 modules compile in under 10\,s under
Thorough, 29 under Fast. Zlib's \texttt{trees.c} is an outlier which takes
1485\,s under Thorough and 496\,s under Fast; Figure~\ref{fig:modules}
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app-full}) shows the full per-module distribution.
The two dials trade that cost for proofs. \emph{Fast} keeps 380 of
Thorough's 885 proofs (\pct{43}) at a third of zlib's cost. 

\paragraph{Whole-compile cost.}
The latency that matters for a developer is the entire compile pipeline's time, not of
one optimizer run. A build runs a front end, the optimizer, and code
generation, and the front end is often a large share of it: Swift's, for
instance, runs its own optimizer before LLVM sees the code. Comparing
against \texttt{-O3} alone would therefore overstate the cost. We timed
every stage of the two pipelines the runtime experiments use, per module,
on the 13 benchmarks whose pipeline can take the pass output: the baseline (optimizing front end, \texttt{-O3}, code
generation) and the same pipeline with \tool{} sandwiched between two 
\texttt{-O3} runs. The median whole compile with \tool{} takes $1.46\times$ the
time of the baseline under Fast and $4.39\times$ under Thorough
(geometric means $2.33\times$ and $4.75\times$; ranges
$1.29$--$65.5\times$ and $1.49$--$209\times$). These are the numbers a
developer actually waits for, front end and code generation included,
and they are reasonable for an online pass: a typical build with Fast takes
little more than a conventional one, and even Thorough stays within a
small factor of it. 

\paragraph{Parallelism and timeouts.}
Both dials make both configurations faster (Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}, zlib's
\texttt{deflate.c}). A strict per-query budget costs few proofs: \pct{98}
of them survive 1\,s and \pct{91} survive 100\,ms, because most queries
finish in milliseconds (Figure~\ref{fig:latency},
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-full}). Parallelism has a limit: discovery, fact
import, and the many queries guard synthesis poses for each trap run
largely in series, so threads and budgets together speed \tool{} up by
at most $2.0\times$, reached at four workers. The figure is measured
without inductive encoding, whose fact import is also serial and would
confound the two dials.

\paragraph{How to use \tool{}.}
A developer can use either configuration in the edit--compile loop,
since builds recompile only the module that changed and per-module
latency is modest: the median module takes 5.0\,s (zlib) and 1.4\,s
(zstd) under Thorough, and the libraries' main modules under half a
minute. For larger modules and whole code bases, Fast remains cheap ---
every kernel in under 2.3\,s, zstd and CryptoSwift in 33\,s and 70\,s;
where Thorough is too slow, the developer iterates with Fast and builds
the final binary with Thorough, the same trade release builds already
make with link-time and profile-guided optimization. Fast is not a
straw man: where it keeps Thorough's proofs it keeps Thorough's
speedups, $1.336\times$ on Rust matmul (Table~\ref{tab:fast},
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-full}).

\subsection{RQ4: How does super-analysis compare with superoptimization?}
\label{sec:results-souper}
For the scope of this study we compare one representative of each paradigm, \tool{} with Souper~\cite{sasnauskas2017souper}, the
synthesizing superoptimizer for LLVM IR, on the task this paper targets:
removing the safety checks of the two C libraries. We ran Souper in its
\emph{constant mode}, which only proves that a value is always the same
constant. The authors of Souper used this mode as an online optimizer, and compiled LLVM's own code base using it. In a smaller scale experiment,
we also ran Souper in its CEGIS synthesis mode.

\paragraph{Setup.} Souper pins LLVM~18, whose parser cannot read the IR
of newer front ends, so the comparison covers only the C libraries which can be compiled
with LLVM~18's clang. Each module is emitted by clang~18 and both tools read the emitted IR;
\tool{}'s runs used a packaged clang~18 whose output leaves exactly the same
checks as Souper's build (476 in zlib, 254 and 251 in zstd). \tool{}'s LLVM reads it as bitcode, which newer LLVM
versions are guaranteed to accept. Each tool runs with its own pinned
LLVM and its own pinned Z3
(Souper: Z3 4.13; \tool{}: Z3 4.8.12), is followed by its own LLVM's \texttt{-O3}, and is compared
against that LLVM's \texttt{-O3} alone. Souper's per-query budget is
10\,s, as for Thorough, with no external cache; its in-memory cache of
identical queries is left on, as shipped. Swift, Rust, and Julia are
excluded because their front ends emit IR newer than LLVM~18.

Thorough and Fast use ten
threads.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relwork}
\S\ref{sec:related} placed super-analysis among the paradigms of
aggressive optimization; here we compare it with work that shares its
tools or its target.

\paragraph{Combining analyses.}
Cousot and Cousot's reduced product~\cite{cousot1979systematic} combines abstract domains; Wegman and Zadeck~\cite{wegman1991constant} and Click and Cooper~\cite{click1995combining} combine specific analyses by hand; Lerner et al.~\cite{lerner2002composing} compose them automatically into a super-analysis; and Gulwani and Tiwari~\cite{gulwani2006combining} combine abstract interpreters through a logical product, in the tradition of the theory combination that underlies SMT solvers. \tool{} uses an SMT solver directly as the combiner, applied to the facts that existing compiler analyses already compute.

\paragraph{SMT for compiler analysis.}
Closer in spirit to our work is Taneja et al.~\cite{taneja2020knownbits},
who use an SMT solver to test LLVM's dataflow analyses. For each expression they compute the
\emph{most precise} answer an analysis could possibly give --- the
exact set of known bits, the tightest integer range --- and compare it
with LLVM's. LLVM's answers are sound but often imprecise: only \pct{48.7} of its integer ranges and
\pct{35.0} of its demanded-bits results are as tight as possible. Their solver-based analysis
is slow --- 9\,s per expression for known bits, 16\,s for integer
ranges --- and they conclude it is ``too slow to use at compile time'', and is not viable as an online optimizer.
The cost comes from the question they ask. Finding the best abstract
value is a \emph{search} over the abstract domain: known bits takes
$2n$ solver queries for an $n$-bit value, and ranges take a binary
search, so one answer costs dozens of queries. \tool{} asks a different
and much cheaper question: one yes/no reachability query per check
site (decision rather than search), which is why our median query
takes milliseconds (\S\ref{sec:results-cost}).
Alive2~\cite{lopes2021alive2} applies a solver to compiler
\emph{correctness} rather than precision: it validates LLVM's
transformations by checking refinement between the IR before and after
each pass. Like \tool{}, it treats unencoded behavior as an
over-approximation, so a solver verdict is only trusted in one
direction.

\paragraph{Bounds-check elimination and sanitizer overhead.}
Removing redundant safety checks is a classic problem.
ABCD~\cite{bodik2000abcd} proves bounds checks redundant by solving
difference constraints over an extended SSA form,
HotSpot~\cite{wurthinger2007hotspot} eliminates them with range analysis
and loop hoisting, and LLVM's inductive range-check elimination splits a
loop around checks whose index is affine in the induction variable. Each
reasons in a single abstract domain chosen in advance; the proofs \tool{}
needs combine several domains at once (\S\ref{sec:conjunction}) and, for
heap-resident lengths, memory (\S\ref{sec:frame}). For sanitizers such as
AddressSanitizer~\cite{serebryany2012asan} and UBSan~\cite{ubsanDocs},
ASAP~\cite{wagner2015asap} removes the checks on the hottest code under a
user-chosen overhead budget, trading safety for speed, and
SanRazor~\cite{zhang2021sanrazor} removes checks it finds redundant with
other checks, using profiling as well as static analysis. \tool{} removes
only checks it proves can never fire, each backed by a solver proof.

\paragraph{Guards and loop versioning.}
Cloning a loop behind a runtime test is established practice: .NET's
RyuJIT clones loops to remove bounds checks, and LLVM's loop versioning
guards a copy by runtime alias checks. In both, the guard is taken from a
fixed syntactic pattern. Guard synthesis (\S\ref{sec:guards}) instead
obtains the guard from the solver, as an abductive
hypothesis~\cite{dillig2012abductive,dillig2013abductive}, and verifies
that it is sufficient and consistent before the loop is cloned.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We introduced super-analysis, an alternative paradigm of aggressive compiler optimization which uses a solver for analysis only. We show that this paradigm is effective: it can speed up our benchmarks up to $4.01\times$ on x86. We also show that it is relatively fast: Super-analysis can be used online, and the cost of compilation can be controlled by parallelization and strict SMT timeout budgets. Our implementation, \tool{}, targets safety check removal and loop cloning under synthesized guards. Future work consists of extending this paradigm into other analyses and optimizations.

\section{Residue: What the Current Fact Sources Cannot Prove}
\label{sec:future}
The checks \tool{} leaves behind are not a random remainder. They fall
into four classes, each defined by the \emph{kind} of fact that is
missing rather than by the benchmark it appears in.

\paragraph{(a) Symbolic trip counts --- solved.}
A loop counter bounded by an SSA-valued expression rather than a
constant. Dominance-based guard collection cannot see the bound, because
the back-edge test that establishes it does not dominate the check. The
\textsc{scevsym} fact source (\S\ref{sec:system-facts}) supplies it:
umin-exact for single-exit loops, a symbolic maximum for multi-exit
loops, exact division for strided counts. This class is closed.

\paragraph{(b) Heap and interprocedural length invariants.}
The length that bounds an index lives in memory, not in a register:
Swift's \texttt{array.count} on a runtime allocation, Rust's \texttt{Vec}
length behind opaque provenance, Julia's array-header \texttt{size}
field on an argument. Each load of it is a fresh SMT variable, so a
guard that constrains one load says nothing about the next. Heap
invariance (\S\ref{sec:frame}) closes the intraprocedural case where the
two loads read a provably unclobbered location --- this is what takes
stdlib GEMM from 0 to 9 of 16 --- but the interprocedural and
call-carried cases remain.

\paragraph{(c) Anchor gaps --- solved.}
One check site reached by several predecessors, or several sites sharing
one error block, so that a single query has to hold on paths with
incompatible contexts. Splitting the obligation per incoming edge, and
allowing partial elimination of a shared error block, closes it.

\paragraph{(d) Non-unit and variable stride induction.}
Scalar evolution computes no trip count at all, in any form, for a loop
whose back-edge test compares an unsigned counter advancing by a stride greater
than one --- base64 at stride 3, crc32 at stride 4, UTF-8 validation at
a data-dependent stride of 1 to 4. No amount of trip-count machinery
helps, because there is no trip count to translate. The remedy is to
stop asking for one: encode the induction structurally, as a base case
plus a step case over a copy of the loop body
(\S\ref{sec:app-machinery}). That closes crc32's four
stride-4 bounds checks; base64 and UTF-8 remain, the former because its
residue is also class~(b). Guard synthesis (\S\ref{sec:guards}) reaches
base64's hot checks instead, in a guarded copy of the loop.

\section{Definitional Encoding of Loop Bounds}
\label{sec:app-defs}
Two refinements make trip-count facts usable on vectorized loops, each
replacing a free variable with the definition of the code that computes
it. First,
trip-count bounds are stated in \emph{subtraction form},
\[
\den{\phi} - \mathit{start} \ \le_u\ s \cdot \mathrm{BTC}
\qquad\text{instead of}\qquad
\den{\phi} \ \le_u\ \mathit{start} + s \cdot \mathrm{BTC},
\]
where $\phi$ is a loop-header phi, $s$ its stride, and $\mathrm{BTC}$
the loop's back-edge-taken count. Modular subtraction cancels overflow
exactly, so the unit-stride bound needs no no-wrap side condition and
holds for the symbolic start values of remainder loops. Second, the
values these bounds mention (the vectorizer's resume indices and the code
that computes them) are encoded through the ordinary rules of
Table~\ref{tab:encoding} instead of as free variables. No ``trip count
$>0$'' is asserted anywhere: the countermodels that stand inside
zero-trip loops die because they violate these definitions. Each
addition holds in every execution, so soundness is unaffected. Neither
is specific to one benchmark; stdlib GEMM, whose loops the vectorizer
splits into vector, remainder, and runtime-checked versions, is where
they matter most: with the heap facts of \S\ref{sec:frame-machinery},
they take it from 0 to 9 of its 16 trap edges.
