\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{abstract}
Aggressive compiler optimization today occupies two distant extremes.
Conventional optimizers run in milliseconds but reason through ad-hoc
heuristics that miss opportunities; superoptimizers synthesize faster code
but take hours, confining them to small kernels and offline use. We introduce
\emph{super-analysis}, a middle ground that uses an SMT solver purely for
\emph{analysis} while transformations remain conventional, avoiding
program synthesis entirely. The key argument for super-analysis is that many simple, yet fruitful transformations require complex reasoning: proving a safety check dead often needs facts from four
independent LLVM analyses \emph{simultaneously} --- a conjunctive entailment
problem suited to a solver, not to any single dataflow pass.


We instantiate
super-analysis in \tool{}, an LLVM pass that proves sanitizer traps and
native-language safety checks unreachable and removes them.
One mechanism serves four frontends and yields speedups in each:
\tool{} generates code up to $3.91\times$ faster than LLVM -O3 for a GEMM implemented in Julia.
A further solver-based optimization, \emph{guard synthesis}, handles
checks that are not dead for every input: the solver synthesizes a cheap invariant safety guard under which a fast check-free loop is cloned,
speeding up a Swift Base64 encoder by $1.44\times$ and a Julia LZ77
compressor by $4.01\times$. Compared with Souper, the synthesizing
superoptimizer for LLVM, \tool{} removes more safety checks and is orders of magnitude faster in most configurations, making it viable as an online compiler pass.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Compiler optimization research today occupies two distant extremes of a
cost-precision spectrum. At one end, conventional optimizers such as
LLVM's O3 pipeline~\cite{lattner2004llvm} make millions of decisions in
milliseconds, each justified by an ad-hoc heuristic or a lightweight
dataflow analysis; the heuristics are fast precisely because each one
reasons locally and gives up early. At the
other end, superoptimizers~\cite{massalin1987superoptimizer,
schkufza2013stoke, sasnauskas2017souper} hand the entire problem ---
analysis \emph{and} transformation --- to an expensive decision procedure
that aggressively searches for replacement code, achieving precision that heuristics
cannot match at costs that confine them to peephole windows and offline
runs.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{spectrum.pdf}
\caption{The cost--precision spectrum of aggressive compiler
optimization. Conventional pipelines are online but heuristic and
incomplete; superoptimizers are precise but offline. Super-analysis
occupies the unpopulated middle: on-demand SMT proofs at bounded latency,
with no program synthesis, inside the compilation pipeline.}
\Description{A horizontal axis from online/low-latency on the left to
offline/high-latency on the right. Three boxes sit along it: Conventional
Optimization (LLVM -O3) on the left, Super-Analysis (ODeSSy) in the
middle, and Superoptimization (Souper, STOKE) on the right.}
\label{fig:spectrum}
\end{figure}
This paper argues for a middle ground we call \emph{super-analysis}
(Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}): use
the decision procedure for \emph{analysis only}, and leave transformation
to the conventional machinery that already exists. No program is synthesized;
no search over programs is performed. The solver answers exactly one kind of question
--- \emph{is this program point reachable under this context?} --- and a
standard, trivially correct transformation (deleting an unreachable branch)
consumes the answer.
We use an SMT solver as an analysis oracle and feed it with the encoded formula $Context \land Query$ and ask if it is satisfiable. Soundness is preserved by construction: an
\textsc{unsat} verdict is a proof, a \textsc{sat} or \textsc{unknown}
verdict changes nothing.


Why should a solver find opportunities that forty years of heuristics
miss? Our central empirical finding is that the missed opportunities require complex reasoning  over multiple domains and from different fact sources to prove safe. Proving
one bounds check dead in a real SHA-256 kernel requires the return-value
range of one function, a scalar-evolution loop bound, a value-range fact
on a third value, and two dominating branch conditions from the program's prefix ---
\emph{simultaneously}. Each fact lives in a different LLVM analysis; no
single pass computes their conjunction, and the phase-ordering problem
ensures no fixed pipeline ever will. Deciding satisfiability of a
complex conjunction is, in principle, an entailment
problem --- exactly what an SMT solver decides and exactly what a
heuristic cannot. Our tool extracts the unsat core of every proof, so this
claim is not an intuition but a measurement: the cores name the
contributing analyses, and the majority of our nontrivial proofs draw on
two to four sources at once (\S\ref{sec:results-static}).


We instantiate super-analysis in \tool{}\footnote{On-Demand SMT System.},
an LLVM module pass targeting one of the most consequential class of
provably-dead code in modern software: \emph{safety checks}. Sanitizers
such as UBSan~\cite{ubsanDocs} and the native checks of memory-safe
languages (Swift bounds and overflow checks, Rust panics, Julia bounds
errors) compile to trap-guarded branches whose hot-path cost is the
standing tax of memory safety --- we measure it at up to $1.075\times$ on
the zstd library under signed-overflow checks and up to $4.17\times$ and $5.11\times$ on native-language kernels (Julia GEMM and Swift nbody, respectively).
Today the only recourse on hot paths is to turn checks \emph{off}
wholesale (\texttt{-Ounchecked}, \texttt{--check-bounds=no}), trading away
the safety guarantee entirely. \tool{} offers the sound alternative:
delete exactly the checks that are provably redundant with the guards,
contracts, and invariants already present in the program, and keep every
check that is not --- each deletion certified by an unsat core, audited
for vacuity, and validated by byte-identical program output.
\tool{} is able to recover up to $3.91\times$ of performance by removing redundant safety checks and enabling a cascade of further optimizations.

Deleting checks that are already dead is not \tool{}'s only
optimization. Many hot checks are not dead for every input: they are
dead only for the well-formed inputs a program actually receives, such
as arrays of the expected length. For these, \tool{} performs
\emph{guard synthesis}. The solver synthesizes a guard --- a cheap
condition over values that do not change inside the loop --- under
which every check in the loop is provably dead. The loop is then
specialized: the guard is tested once, outside the loop, and when it
holds a copy of the loop runs with all its checks removed; otherwise
the original loop runs unchanged. Because real inputs take the
specialized copy, the hot loop becomes the fast one. Guard synthesis
stays within the paradigm: the solver synthesizes a condition, not a
program, and the transformation is an ordinary loop clone. On x86 it
speeds up a Swift Base64 encoder by $1.44\times$ and a Julia LZ77
compressor by $4.01\times$.

Super-analysis is a general compilation paradigm rather than a single
optimization, and it carries one open problem we deliberately defer:
\emph{when is expensive analysis worth invoking?} A solver consulted
indiscriminately at every program point would be ruinous, and deciding
where precision repays its cost demands a cost model no compiler has.
Safety-check elimination is the ideal first instantiation precisely
because it answers that question for free. The work list is given --- run
exactly where the traps are, a set the frontend already enumerates. Based on our experiments, removing a safety check is either a win or has a marginal effect in most cases --- it simplifies the program control-flow, allowing other optimizations to be performed by subsequent passes. This
application therefore isolates the paradigm's contribution --- precision
of analysis --- from the question it defers: when to use it.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Super-analysis} (\S\ref{sec:superanalysis}): an aggressive compilation paradigm
  positioning between conventional heuristic-based optimization and superoptimization in terms of compiler latency and precision. The general recipe for this paradigm is to use solvers as an analysis oracle, while applying cheap conventional rules-based transformation for optimization. We identify heap reasoning as
  a crucial analysis problem --- array optimizations often require heap
  reasoning to ensure their safety --- formalize it, and show that
  super-analysis can more effectively reason over heap and memory safety
  of certain transformations in hot loops (\S\ref{sec:frame}).
\item \textbf{Making super-analysis work} (\S\ref{sec:making}): a set of
  techniques inside the paradigm that make it effective. We encode the
  compiler's lightweight native analyses as facts that constrain the
  solver's search (\S\ref{sec:conjunction}), and we
  introduce guard synthesis and loop specialization, which synthesize a
  runtime guard under which a loop's checks are dead and clone the loop
  behind it (\S\ref{sec:guards}).
\item \textbf{\tool{}} (\S\ref{sec:system}): an LLVM pass and online optimizer
  as an instantiation of super-analysis. \tool{} eliminates sanitizers and safety checks and unlocks further optimizations. It supports C/C++, Swift, Rust, and Julia as frontends and can enable hot-path transformations and speedups.
\item \textbf{Robust Performance Improvement} (\S\ref{sec:results}):
  our systematic evaluation of \tool{} on two target architectures (x86 and M-series) show removing hot-loop checks has a cascading effect, unlocking further transformations (unrolling, vectorization, etc) that enables up to $4.01\times$ speedups for widely used kernels and libraries. \tool{} proofs are generally cheap: the median proof takes 23\,ms, and \pct{96} of
  zlib's proofs survive a 1\,s per-query budget. Multi-threading and per-query budgets make \tool{}'s analysis up to $5.7\times$ faster, and its budget is a dial between an online pass and a release build (\S\ref{sec:results-cost}). A comparison with a synthesizing superoptimizer shows that super-analysis removes several times more checks, in most configurations in less time (\S\ref{sec:results-souper}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Paradigms of Aggressive Compiler Optimization}
\label{sec:related}

Conventional compiler optimization passes (like in LLVM) consist of analysis modules and transformation rules that consume the analysis to produce more efficient code. Both components are lightweight and fast, sacrificing precision to save compile time and provide rapid feedback. Multiple families of optimizers deviate from this simple fast compilation methodology, including superoptimizers and equality saturation methods.

\paragraph{Superoptimization.} A superoptimizer tries to search a large space of programs for a faster or shorter alternative to the initial code.
Massalin's original superoptimizer~\cite{massalin1987superoptimizer}
enumerated every instruction sequence up to a length bound and kept the
shortest one that matched the target on test inputs. Two lines of work
made the idea provably sound and practical. STOKE~\cite{schkufza2013stoke} replaces
enumeration with stochastic search: a random walk
mutates x86-64 kernels, scores each candidate by test-case correctness
and estimated latency, and hands the survivors to an SMT solver for a
formal equivalence proof, at costs of hours per loop-free kernel.
Souper~\cite{sasnauskas2017souper} works at the LLVM IR level: it
synthesizes replacements for bounded peephole windows and verifies each
candidate with an SMT solver. Souper is architecturally different from our work.
In every sound superoptimizer the search proposes code and the solver
verifies it, so the solver is invoked \emph{per candidate}. \tool{}
performs no synthesis at all: its solver decides reachability over
arbitrarily structured acyclic slices, once per check site, and the
compiler's own machinery performs the rewrite. This is why
super-analysis scales to whole libraries (156k-line IR modules; 19{,}197
checks in zstd) at the cost of one decision per check. 


\paragraph{Equality Saturation.}
Equality
saturation~\cite{tate2009equality} searches the same kind of space by a
different route: rather than synthesizing candidates it saturates an
e-graph with the combinations of a fixed rewrite-rule set and extracts
the cheapest result, so its search ranges over rules rather than over
arbitrary programs and no candidate needs a proof of its own.

\paragraph{Super-analysis.} This paper introduces a third route. A
super-analyzer spends the solver on \emph{analysis} alone: the solver
decides whether a rewrite is licensed --- here, whether a safety check
can ever fire --- and the compiler's existing, conventional
transformations perform the rewrite. No replacement code is searched
for, synthesized, or verified. Figure~\ref{fig:quadrants} places the
three paradigms on one map, by what each spends on analysis and what it
spends on transformation; \S\ref{sec:superanalysis} develops the
paradigm.

\section{Super-Analysis}
\label{sec:superanalysis}
\subsection{The design space}
\label{sec:design-space}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{quadrants.pdf}
\caption{Every optimizer is an
analysis that licenses a rewrite plus a transformation that performs it,
and each can be cheap or expensive. Conventional optimization keeps both
cheap; superoptimization makes both expensive. Super-analysis takes one
asymmetric corner where a solver licenses rewrites. Super-transformation takes the other: exhaustive search over
combinations of rewrites. Both asymmetric corners
are under-explored; this paper develops the first.}
\Description{A two-by-two matrix. Columns: transformation cost, cheap
versus expensive. Rows: analysis cost, cheap versus expensive. Cheap/cheap
is Conventional Optimization (LLVM -O3). Cheap analysis with expensive
transformation is Super-Transformation (e.g., equality saturation), marked
under-explored. Expensive analysis with cheap transformation is
Super-Analysis (ODeSSy). Expensive/expensive is Superoptimization (Souper,
STOKE).}
\label{fig:quadrants}
\end{figure}
Conventional optimization and superoptimization differ in \emph{which
component} pays for precision. Every optimizer is two things: an
\emph{analysis} that establishes the facts licensing a rewrite, and a
\emph{transformation} that produces the replacement code. Either can be
cheap or expensive, which gives the four-way design space of
Figure~\ref{fig:quadrants}. A conventional pass has cheap analysis and
cheap transformation, and both are heuristic. A superoptimizer makes both
expensive: the search proposes code, the solver verifies it. Super-analysis
splits the difference asymmetrically: expensive, precise, \emph{sound}
analysis; cheap, conventional transformation. Analysis is partially provided by an expensive SMT query, licensing known transformations. Super-analysis can be strategically used by a compiler to manage the high latency of the solver, making it online and keeping it in the programmer's rapid develop and test loop. As we show in \S\ref{sec:results} removing a safety check in a hot loop by proving it unreachable can unlock valuable transformations (unrolling, vectorization, etc.)

\paragraph{The other asymmetric corner.}
The remaining cell --- cheap analysis, expensive transformation --- is
the mirror image of super-analysis, and we call it
\emph{super-transformation}: an exhaustive search for alternative code.
Synthesis, as practiced by superoptimizers, is its most aggressive form,
because the candidates are arbitrary programs. Equality
saturation~\cite{tate2009equality} is its disciplined form: it exhausts
the sequences and combinations of a fixed set of rewrite rules, which is
still an exhaustive search for replacement code, but over a space every
point of which is correct by construction. That is what keeps equality
saturation in the cheap-analysis row. Each rule is an equality proven
sound once, offline, so no candidate requires a proof of its own at
compile time. The expense is entirely on the transformation side: saturating
the e-graph and then extracting the cheapest term from it, which the
original work posed to a pseudo-boolean solver and later systems solve
greedily or with ILP. Synthesis lands in the superoptimization cell
precisely because it lacks this guarantee: an arbitrary candidate carries
no proof, so each one must be verified, and the analysis becomes
expensive as well.
The matrix therefore states the thesis of this paper exactly.
Superoptimization is super-analysis \emph{plus} super-transformation; we
take the first half and leave the second to the compiler's existing
rules. Both asymmetric corners are under-explored relative to the two
diagonal ones.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=odessy,

; P: the program
Superoptimize(p):         ; p: a point in P
  frag := Window(P, p)    ; code around p
  best := frag
  while budget left:      ; open-ended
    cand := Synthesize(best)    ; search
    if Verify(cand == frag):    ; solver query
      if Cost(cand) < Cost(best): 
        best := cand        
  P := Replace(P, frag, best)
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.50\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=odessy,
    morekeywords={while,if,return,for,each,in,not},comment={[l]{;}}]
; P: the program
SuperAnalyze(p):          ; p: a point in P
  fact := Analyze(P, p)   ; cheap, as today
  if not fact:            ; heuristics failed
    fact := Decide(Context(P, p) && Query(p))
                          ; a bounded number
                          ; of solver queries
  if fact:
    P := Rewrite(P, p)    ; conventional
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}
\caption{The two paradigms as procedures, each applied to one point
$p$ of the program $P$; a compiler runs either one at every point of a
work list it chooses (for \tool{}, every safety check). At a point, a
superoptimizer takes the surrounding fragment and searches over
candidate replacements, paying a solver query for every candidate it
verifies; the search has no natural end and is cut off by a budget, and
the best candidate replaces the fragment. A super-analyzer first asks
the conventional analysis, consults the solver only if that fails, poses
a bounded number of queries, each under its own time budget, and hands
the answer to a conventional rewrite.}
\Description{Two pseudocode functions side by side, each taking a point p
of the program P. Left, Superoptimize: take the fragment around p, then
loop while budget remains, synthesizing a candidate, verifying with one
solver query that it equals the fragment, and keeping it if cheaper;
finally replace the fragment in P by the best candidate. Right,
SuperAnalyze: run the conventional analysis at p; only if it fails, ask
the solver to decide p's context together with its query; if a fact is
established, apply a conventional rewrite to P.}
\label{fig:procedures}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:procedures} states the difference as two procedures.
Both are applied point by point, and they differ in what happens at a
point. The superoptimizer runs a search: its cost is the number of
candidates it tries times the cost of verifying each, the number of
candidates grows with the size of the fragment, and nothing bounds it
except the budget. The super-analyzer has no search at all. The points
it visits come from a work list that the compiler chooses, which makes
its use of the solver \emph{strategic} --- the compiler decides where
precision is worth paying for --- and the solver is consulted only
where the conventional analysis has already failed, which makes it
\emph{surgical}: expensive reasoning is spent on exactly the points
that need it, a bounded number of queries each, every query under a
fixed budget. The transformation that follows is the compiler's own.

The cost consequence is concrete. A
superoptimizer's dominant expense is not the solver but the
\emph{synthesis loop} that feeds it: a search over candidate programs,
each candidate requiring its own equivalence query. Super-analysis deletes
that outer loop. What remains is one decision procedure invocation per
check site --- a decision, not a search --- and a transformation the
compiler performs for free. This is why the two paradigms differ by orders
of magnitude in latency while sharing the same underlying technology, and
why super-analysis can run inside the pipeline rather than beside it
(\S\ref{sec:results-cost}).

The same asymmetry decides how much of the program each paradigm can
see. A synthesizing search must enumerate or sample candidate
replacements, and the space of candidates grows exponentially with the
size of the code being replaced, so superoptimizers confine themselves
to small, mostly local windows: a peephole, a basic block, a loop-free
kernel. A decision procedure faces no such space. It decides a single
formula, so the context it is given can be as large as the analysis
can encode: many basic blocks and their merge points, every dominating
branch condition, and facts imported from loop and heap analyses that
summarize code it never sees. Super-analysis can therefore reason
\emph{globally} about the point it decides, while superoptimization
reasons locally about the code it rewrites; the checks of
\S\ref{sec:conjunction} and \S\ref{sec:frame}, whose proofs combine
facts from several analyses and from code far from the check, are of
the first kind.
\subsection{Encoding program context}
\label{sec:encoding}
A super-analyzer decides a property of a single \emph{program point} ---
here, whether a trap is reachable. But the property is never decided by
the point itself; it is decided by the \emph{context} that leads there.
The core machinery of the paradigm is therefore a translation: render
the context of a program point as a logical formula, and ask the solver
whether the property is consistent with it.

The translation proceeds \emph{backwards} from the point of interest.
In this paper we are concerned safety checks and trap conditions, so starting at the trap condition, it walks the chain of definitions that
feed it and the branch conditions that dominate it, and renders each
instruction as an exact equation over bit-vectors --- arithmetic,
comparisons, shifts, overflow intrinsics, and the control flow
connecting them. The resulting formula is satisfiable exactly by the
machine states that can reach the point, so reachability becomes one
satisfiability query: $\mathit{context} \wedge \mathit{trap}$.

A backward encoding cannot descend forever. It stops at
\emph{boundaries} --- loop-carried values, function calls, loads:
boxes whose interior it does not open. What crosses a boundary is a
\emph{post-condition}: a fact true of the box's result in every
execution. The weakest post-condition is no constraint at all --- the
value is a free variable --- and this is the soundness floor: anything
not understood is over-approximated, so an \textsc{unsat} verdict holds
for every concrete behavior of the unencoded parts. From that floor,
post-condition derivation is a precision dial that can in principle be
turned arbitrarily far: a range, an algebraic relation among boundary
values, a loop invariant, a full summary. 

This encoded context is the dominant machinery of super-analysis: by
proof count, most eliminations in this paper are discharged by the
translated context alone (the \emph{light} tier of
\S\ref{sec:system-facts}). What remains is the boxes the encoding
leaves closed --- which post-conditions to import for them, and why
composing those facts requires a solver.
\subsection{Heap-Invariant Super-Analysis}
\label{sec:frame}
Many of the crucial compiler analysis including alias analysis require heap reasoning. In our particular application, heap facts can be important as the metadata for arrays (e.g. length) often live in the heap, and array index in bound checks cause high performance loss in hot loops. This necessitates super-analysis to perform complex yet fast heap reasoning so it can preserve effectiveness and its status as online analysis at the same time.

The facts a super-analyzer composes are usually \emph{scalar}:
ranges, known bits, trip counts, branch conditions
(\S\ref{sec:conjunction} shows how a compiler supplies them). A large and
expensive class of checks falls outside their reach because the fact
that discharges them lives in the heap. Figure~\ref{fig:frame-example}
shows the pattern. An array's length lives in its header, in memory, so
every read of it is a load. A loop reads the length once to bound its
counter and again inside the loop for the check, and between the two
reads sits a store. The two loads read the same address, but they are
two different values to the compiler: they are equal only if the store
never overwrites the length, which is a question for alias and heap
analysis, not for the scalar facts. The pattern is common in widely
used kernels. In Julia's standard-library GEMM
(\texttt{generic\_matmatmul!}) the scalar fact sources prove \emph{zero}
of 16 residual bounds-check edges, while turning those same checks off
is worth roughly $4\times$ ($4.17\times$ on x86, $4.19\times$ on Apple
Silicon; \S\ref{sec:frame-results}). The checks are not exotic --- they
guard an ordinary three-loop matrix multiply, and Julia's own authors
discharge them by hand, with trusted \texttt{@inbounds} annotations, in
the shipping standard library.

The failure is structural, and not limited to this special GEMM
implementation, as the encoder additions this section motivates also
removed checks in other benchamrks (Swift SHA-256, Julia SHA-256, the 
CryptoSwift library, etc.) 

The fact that
would discharge the check is that certain important metadata of arrays are not overwritten by the stores in the loop. A dataflow analysis
propagates facts \emph{about values}; this premise is a fact
\emph{about memory}. The GEMM implementation consists of multiple loads of arrays' dimensions (stored in the array header in the heap) and multiple stores that the compiler has to prove do not overwrite the metadata between two loads. LLVM's dataflow heuristics can no longer justify the unification
itself --- which is precisely why the checks survive O3, and why a
sound encoder that reads the same IR is stuck for the same reason.

A heap fact alone is also not enough. The proof must \emph{compose} it
with the scalar ones: the flagship GEMM edge needs the load equality
\emph{and} a symbolic trip-count bound \emph{and} known-bits
\emph{and} three phi-image facts simultaneously
(\S\ref{sec:frame-results}). This is the conjunction argument of
\S\ref{sec:conjunction} again, now spanning two theories rather than
one --- which is why the answer is a new \emph{fact source} for the
solver rather than a new pass.

To perform heap invariant super-analysis we need a new fact source: when the
compiler's own memory analyses can show that no store between two
loads touches the location, the encoder may state that the two loads
are equal. This is the frame rule of separation
logic~\cite{reynolds2002separation}, used as a source of facts rather
than as a proof system, and it refuses whenever the memory analyses
cannot answer. The solver still only
answers reachability and the compiler still performs every rewrite,
so the super-analysis contract of \S\ref{sec:superanalysis} holds
unchanged. \S\ref{sec:frame-machinery} gives the rules.

\section{Making Super-Analysis Work}
\label{sec:making}
\S\ref{sec:superanalysis} gave the recipe in its purest form:
encode the program's semantics around a point of interest, and ask the
solver. The recipe is sound, but on its own it is rarely enough to be
useful. The facts a proof needs often hide inside boxes the encoding
does not open, and some checks are simply not dead for every input. This section collects the techniques that make
super-analysis practical and useful. Like
\S\ref{sec:superanalysis}, it is a general recipe, independent of
LLVM and applicable to any compiler: we import the lightweight facts a
compiler already computes, to constrain the solver's search
(\S\ref{sec:conjunction}), and when a check
cannot be proved dead for every execution, we synthesize a guard under
which it is dead and clone the loop behind it (\S\ref{sec:guards}).
Throughout, every solver query runs under a fixed time budget, so a
hard query costs a missed proof, never an unbounded compile
(\S\ref{sec:results-cost}). \S\ref{sec:system} shows how \tool{}
realizes each technique in LLVM, and the evaluation maps each one to
its results.

\subsection{Encoding Lightweight Heuristics}
\label{sec:conjunction}
Encoding the context alone does not produce every useful proof. The
encoding stops at boundaries, and a boundary value is free: the solver
may pick any value for it, including values no execution can produce.
Yet the compiler has usually learned something about that value
already. Its lightweight, fast native analyses --- value ranges, known
bits, loop trip counts --- run in milliseconds, and each describes a
slice of the truth. Importing their results as facts about the boundary
values constrains the solver's search space to executions that can
really happen. No single one of these facts is usually enough, though:
a proof needs several of them at once, and composing them is exactly
where a solver becomes necessary.

(Figure~\ref{fig:simple-example}), whose inner

these accesses survive both \texttt{swiftc -O} and a subsequent full LLVM
O3. \tool{} proves them dead, and the extracted unsat cores show why no
single heuristic could. Each proof's minimal core has the shape:
\[
\textsf{Spec} \wedge \textsf{RM} \wedge \textsf{SCEV} \wedge \textsf{LVI}
\wedge \textsf{SCEV}' \wedge G_0 \wedge G_k \;\vdash\; \bot
\]
--- a return-value range (\textsf{RM}), two scalar-evolution facts
(\textsf{SCEV}), a lattice value range (\textsf{LVI}), and two dominating
guard conditions ($G_0$, $G_k$), jointly inconsistent with the check
firing (\textsf{Spec}). Four independent analyses and path conditions must be intersected;
each in isolation is insufficient (removing any core member returns
\textsc{sat}). LLVM's analyses do not compose this way: each consumes the
IR and produces facts in its own domain, and no pass computes the
satisfiability of their conjunction --- nor could a fixed pipeline, since
which facts matter is different at every check site. Deciding such
conjunctions is the definitional competence of an SMT
solver~\cite{demoura2008z3}. This is the sense in which super-analysis is
\emph{necessary} rather than merely convenient: the reasoning is
entailment over a conjunction of theories, not dataflow over a lattice.
\subsection{Guard Synthesis and Loop Cloning}
\label{sec:guards}
Sometimes a check cannot be proved dead for every execution, because it
is not dead for every execution. Consider the Julia matrix multiply
\texttt{matmul!} in our benchmarks. It takes three flat arrays and a
dimension $n$, and indexes them as \texttt{a[(i-1)*n+k]}. Nothing in the
function ties the lengths of the arrays to $n$: a caller may pass arrays
that are too short, and then the bounds check fires, exactly as it
should. No proof of universal deadness exists, because the claim is
false.

Yet the calls that matter are the well-formed ones, where each array
holds $n^2$ elements, and for those every bounds check in the loop nest
is dead. The property is hard to prove for the loop, but easy to prove
for a \emph{constrained} version of it --- and the constrained version is
the frequent one, often the only one that ever runs.

Guard synthesis exploits this. Instead of asking whether the check is
dead, we ask \emph{under what condition} it is dead: a condition over
values that do not change inside the loop, cheap enough to test once
before the loop starts. Given such a guard, the loop is cloned
(Figure~\ref{fig:guard}). The fast copy has its checks removed; the
original, with every check intact, runs whenever the guard fails. For
\texttt{matmul!} the solver synthesizes
\[
n \le 2^{15} \;\wedge\; |a|, |b|, |c| > n^2 - 1 \;\wedge\;
|a|, |b|, |c| \le 2^{30},
\]
which holds on every correctly sized call we ran, so the fast copy is
the one that always executes.

\end{minipage}

\label{fig:guard}

The transformation itself is simple; the hard part is the guard. It must
be a smart, useful guard: weak enough to hold on the executions we care
about, and loop-invariant and cheap, so that testing it costs nothing
measurable. And it must be sufficient: under the guard, the fast copy
must be provably safe to run. Both are questions about the conjunction
of the guard, the context, and the trap condition, so, again, they
necessitate a solver. The solver proposes the guard, verifies that the
trap is unreachable under it, and verifies that the guard does not
contradict the context --- a contradictory guard would make the fast
copy dead code.

The idea resembles abductive inference~\cite{dillig2012abductive,
dillig2013abductive}. Given known facts $\Gamma$ and a goal $\varphi$,
abduction seeks a hypothesis $\psi$ that is \emph{sufficient} and
\emph{consistent}:
\[
\Gamma \wedge \psi \models \varphi
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\Gamma \wedge \psi \not\models \bot .
\]
Guard synthesis instantiates this directly. $\Gamma$ is the encoded
context of the check, with its dominating guards and imported facts; the
goal is that the trap is unreachable, $\varphi = \neg\,\mathit{trap}$; and
the hypothesis is the guard $H$, restricted to values that do not change
inside the loop. Since $\Gamma \wedge H \models \neg\,\mathit{trap}$
holds exactly when $\Gamma \wedge H \wedge \mathit{trap}$ has no model,
each condition becomes one solver query:
\[
\underbrace{\Gamma \wedge H \wedge \mathit{trap}\ \text{is \textsc{unsat}}}_{\text{sufficient: the fast copy is safe}}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\underbrace{\Gamma \wedge H\ \text{is \textsc{sat}}}_{\text{consistent: the guard is not vacuous}} .
\]
A guard that failed the second query would make the trap unreachable
only because the fast copy itself could never run. Among the guards that
pass both, we prefer the weakest, so that it holds on as many real
inputs as possible.

\section{\tool{}: Design and Implementation}
\label{sec:system}
\tool{} is an LLVM module pass of roughly five thousand lines. Two
requirements dictate its design: soundness under aggressive
parallelism, and bit-reproducible verdicts regardless of thread count.
This section describes the checks \tool{} targets
(\S\ref{sec:target}), the pipeline the pass lives in
(\S\ref{sec:pipeline}), the encoding machinery at its core
(\S\ref{sec:encoding-impl}), inductive loop encoding
(\S\ref{sec:app-machinery}), the heap-invariance extension
(\S\ref{sec:frame-machinery}), guard synthesis and loop cloning
(\S\ref{sec:guards-impl}), and how proofs are audited before they are
believed (\S\ref{sec:audit}).

\subsection{Target: safety checks}
\label{sec:target}
\tool{} targets the \emph{safety checks} that compilers insert into
programs. A safety check guards an operation whose result would
otherwise be undefined or unsafe --- an array access out of bounds, a
signed integer overflow --- with a test and a branch to a block that
stops the program if the test fails. Every front end we support inserts
them. In C and C++, UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer~\cite{ubsanDocs} adds a
check for each kind of undefined behavior it is asked to catch; in trap
mode each one ends in a call to \texttt{llvm.ubsantrap}. Swift checks
every array access and every arithmetic overflow by default, in
optimized builds too, and stops with \texttt{llvm.trap}~\cite{swiftBook}.
Rust checks every slice and array index, and every arithmetic operation
when overflow checks are enabled, and stops with a call to a panic
function~\cite{rustReference}. Julia checks every array access and stops
by throwing a bounds error~\cite{juliaBoundsCheck}. After lowering to
LLVM IR all of them have one shape --- a conditional branch to a block
that calls a function that never returns --- and that shape is what
\tool{} looks for (\S\ref{sec:pipeline}).

The checks make programs safe, and the safety has a price.
Figure~\ref{fig:checks} shows the innermost loop of Julia's
standard-library matrix multiply with one of the checks Julia inserts.
It runs on every iteration of the hottest loop of the program, and as a
side exit from that loop it keeps LLVM from vectorizing and unrolling
the loop as fully as the check-free version: with the checks turned off
this kernel runs $4.17\times$ faster on x86, and Swift's n-body
simulation $5.11\times$. Today the only way to recover that performance
is to turn checks off wholesale (\texttt{@inbounds},
\texttt{--check-bounds=no}, \texttt{-Ounchecked}), giving up the guarantee
for the whole loop or program. \tool{} instead removes only the checks it
proves can never fail, so the program stays exactly as safe; on this
kernel it recovers $3.91\times$ (\S\ref{sec:results-perf}).

\subsection{Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}
\tool{} runs sandwiched between two full \texttt{-O3} pipelines. The
first produces the optimized, check-laden IR that is the analysis
input; the second, after elimination, re-optimizes the simplified
control flow and performs the transformations the deleted checks were
blocking --- unrolling, vectorization (\S\ref{sec:results-mech}). The
pass itself changes nothing but the proven branches: the solver
licenses, the compiler rewrites.

Internally the pass is three stages. \emph{Serial discovery} scans the
module for trap sites: calls to
\texttt{llvm.ubsantrap}/\texttt{llvm.trap} plus, behind a divergence
gate (the callee is no-return, or the call is followed by
\texttt{unreachable}), any callee named in the \texttt{traps=}
parameter --- one mechanism that retargets the pass to Swift, Rust,
and Julia checks with zero per-language code. Each incoming edge of a
trap block becomes one independent \emph{job}
(\S\ref{sec:encoding-impl}); the job list spans the whole module, so
one function with 400 traps and ten with 2 load-balance perfectly.
\emph{Parallel solve}: workers claim jobs in discovery order, each
owning a private Z3 context; the IR is read-only throughout. The only
shared mutable state is LLVM's own analyses, whose internal caches make
concurrent queries both a data race and a determinism hazard --- so
every analysis query passes through a ticket turnstile keyed on
discovery index, serializing them in one canonical order. That single
mechanism buys thread safety \emph{and} reproducibility: verdicts,
output IR, and logs are identical for \texttt{threads=1} and
\texttt{threads=N}, which we verify by \texttt{diff}, as long as no
query lands at its time budget: a wall-clock budget lets contention
decide such a query (Figure~\ref{fig:matrix}). \emph{Serial
transform}: the main thread folds each proven branch to a constant in
discovery order --- the only IR mutation in the pass --- and standard
\texttt{simplifycfg}/\texttt{adce} reclaim the dead blocks. Workers
fence all exceptions and degrade to keeping the trap: every error path
in the system converges on declining to optimize.

The unit of proof is a trap \emph{edge}, fixed by an \emph{anchor}.
Frontends merge error blocks, and loop multiversioning clones checks
around a shared one, so a trap block routinely has many incoming
edges. Each qualifying edge --- a predecessor $P$ ending in a
conditional branch with condition $c$ whose one successor is the trap
block --- is an independent obligation: the trap fires via this edge
iff control reaches $P$ and $c$ holds. A proof folds only $P$'s
branch, so \emph{partial} elimination of a shared trap block is sound
and profitable (one side exit of one loop version disappears).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Encoding and deciding one trap edge $(P, c)$}
\label{alg:encode}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $G \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{dominating context}
\ForAll{dominators $D$ of $P$ ending in a cond.\ branch}
  \If{one outgoing edge of $D$ dominates $P$}
    $G \gets G \cup \{$that edge's condition$\}$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $G \gets G \cup \{$conditions of \texttt{llvm.assume}s dominating $P\}$
\State $S \gets$ backward worklist walk from $c$ and $G$:
\Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent} enter merge-point phis (incoming
  values $+$ region branch conditions);
\Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent} \textbf{stop} at loads, geps,
  unencoded calls, loop-header phis \Comment{boundaries}
\ForAll{$I \in S$ in reverse post-order}
  \State assert $\den{I}$ per Table~\ref{tab:encoding}
  \Comment{boundaries: fresh variables}
\EndFor
\State assert every $g \in G$ (tracked);\ \ [heavy] assert boundary
  facts (\S\ref{sec:system-facts})
\State \Return $\mathrm{check\mbox{-}sat}(c)$
\Comment{\textsc{unsat}: fold edge; \textsc{sat}/\textsc{unknown}: keep}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Encoding a job proceeds backwards from $c$ in three steps
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:encode}).
\emph{(1)~Guards.} Walk the dominator tree upward from $P$: for every
dominator ending in a conditional branch with one outgoing edge that
dominates $P$, every execution reaching $P$ took that edge, so the
edge's condition is asserted as context --- SSA immutability
guarantees its operands still denote the same values at the trap.
Dominating \texttt{llvm.assume} conditions join the same way.
\emph{(2)~Slice.} A worklist walks operands backwards from $c$ and
from every guard. It stops at the \emph{boundaries} of
\S\ref{sec:encoding}: loads, address computations, calls we do not
encode, and loop-header phis, all of which become free variables. A
phi at an ordinary merge point is entered instead: its incoming values
and the branch conditions of its merge region join the slice. Each
value is visited once, and every cyclic dependence in SSA passes
through a loop-header phi --- a boundary --- so the walk terminates
with a slice linear in the function.
\emph{(3)~Translation.} The slice is encoded in reverse post-order by
the rules of Table~\ref{tab:encoding}: one exact bit-vector equation
per instruction, at the instruction's own width. Control flow is
encoded once per block, not once per path: a merge-point phi becomes
an if-then-else chain gated by a memoized reachability formula
\[
\mathit{Reach}(B) \;=\; \textstyle\bigvee_{P \in \mathrm{preds}(B)}
\mathit{Reach}(P) \wedge \mathit{edge}(P{\to}B),
\]
an $O(\mathit{edges})$ circuit whose shared prefixes are stored once
(Z3 terms are hash-consed), leaving path enumeration to the SAT
engine rather than to an exponential DNF.

The query is then $\mathit{guards} \wedge \mathit{definitions} \wedge
c$: satisfiable exactly by the machine states, consistent with
everything encoded, in which this edge reaches the trap.
\textsc{unsat} proves the edge dead in every execution;
\textsc{sat} and \textsc{unknown} (timeout) keep the check.

\subsection{Encoding LLVM's analyses}
\label{sec:system-facts}
The \emph{light} tier is the machinery above: free variables are
constrained only by their types. The \emph{heavy} tier strengthens the
boundaries with post-conditions bought from LLVM's own analyses,
asserted as labeled constraints on the free variables:
\texttt{!range} metadata and range attributes (RM), KnownBits (KB),
LazyValueInfo ranges (LVI), scalar-evolution constant ranges (SCEV),
and a symbolic trip-count translation (SCEVSYM) that renders loop
bounds --- including symbolic starts and an exact unsigned-division
translation --- as constraints on loop-header phis. The import
discipline is refusal: a fact that is trivial, width-mismatched, or
self-contradictory (an empty LVI range, conflicting known bits) is
dropped rather than repaired. The \texttt{ldeq} option adds
load-equality within a basic block --- two loads of the same pointer
with no intervening may-write share one variable, exactly GVN's
theorem replayed at encoding time. Three tiers name the configurations
used throughout the paper: \emph{light}, \emph{heavy} (analysis facts
+ \texttt{ldeq}), and \emph{full} (heavy + the \texttt{frame} facts of
\S\ref{sec:frame-machinery}). The remaining knobs are
\texttt{timeout=} and \texttt{threads=} (the two latency dials of
\S\ref{sec:results-cost}), \texttt{traps=} (\S\ref{sec:pipeline}), and
\texttt{vacuity} (\S\ref{sec:audit}); Appendix~\ref{sec:app-knobs}
lists every knob.

\subsection{Encoding loops inductively}
\label{sec:app-machinery}
Loop-header $\phi$s are boundaries (\S\ref{sec:encoding}), so a check
whose safety depends on how a loop's values evolve is out of reach of a
single query unless a fact source bounds those values. Scalar evolution
often does (SCEVSYM), but not for loops that advance by a variable or
non-unit stride, or exit in several places. The \texttt{ind} option
proves such checks by one-step induction over the loop, with no trip
count and no invariant synthesis. Let $\vec\phi$ be the header $\phi$s
of the trap's loop, $\vec\phi_0$ their entry values, $\Gamma$ the
encoded context of the trap, and primes denote a copy of the loop body
over fresh symbols that stands for the previous iteration. Two queries
are posed:
\[
\textsc{Base}:\ \ \Gamma \wedge \vec\phi = \vec\phi_0 \wedge \mathit{trap}
\qquad
\textsc{Step}:\ \ \Gamma \wedge \Gamma' \wedge \mathit{latch}' \wedge
\vec\phi = \mathit{next}'(\vec\phi') \wedge \neg\,\mathit{trap}' \wedge \mathit{trap}
\]
where $\mathit{latch}'$ states that the previous iteration reached the
latch and took the back edge, $\mathit{next}'$ computes the header
values that edge carries, and $\neg\,\mathit{trap}'$ states that the
check did not fire on the previous iteration. If both are
\textsc{unsat}, the check cannot fire on the first iteration, nor on any
iteration that follows one on which it did not fire; by induction it
never fires. \tool{} poses the two queries only when the direct query is
\textsc{sat} or \textsc{unknown} and the trap lies in a loop, first for
the trap's own loop and then for each enclosing one. The copy's fresh
symbols receive the same facts as ordinary boundaries, except facts
valid only at a single program point. The heavy tier also carries four
syntactic \emph{header-phi interval} rules (\texttt{PHIINV}), each a
fact about a loop-header $\phi$ justified by the same one-step argument
(for example, a counter that only increases stays at or above its start
value); induction recovers 12 of the 14 proofs those rules provide and
adds proofs they cannot state, and the two mechanisms are used together.
Because the copy queries scalar evolution outside the turnstile of
\S\ref{sec:pipeline}, \texttt{ind} runs on one thread.

\subsection{Encoding heap invariance}
\label{sec:frame-machinery}
The \texttt{frame} option implements the requirements of
\S\ref{sec:frame} as two encoder-level additions.
\paragraph{FRAME: the frame rule as a fact source.}
For a candidate pair of loads $L_1$ (guard-side) and $L_2$ (check-side)
of the same address, the imported fact is
\[
\frac{\mathrm{clobber}\bigl(L_2,\ \mathrm{loc}(L_1)\bigr) \ \preceq\
      \mathrm{def}(L_1)}
     {\ |\mathrm{FRAME}{:}k|:\ \ \den{L_1} = \den{L_2}\ }
\]
--- if MemorySSA's clobbering access for $L_2$, queried \emph{at
$L_1$'s own memory location} (address, width, and aliasing metadata),
is $L_1$'s defining access or one dominating it, the loads agree.
Querying with $L_1$'s location is the load-bearing choice: the
check-side loads are vectorizer-cloned and carry no metadata at all
(which is precisely why LLVM leaves the checks in), while the
guard-side load carries the frontend's scoped no-alias metadata, under
which stock \textsc{ScopedNoAliasAA} proves every loop-carried store
harmless. The equality enters the query as a context-side fact labeled
$|\mathrm{FRAME}{:}k|$ --- vacuity-audited, and guarded by two standing
\textsc{sat} tripwire tests (a may-alias clobber and a single-arm
clobber must refuse forever). No new alias analysis is written: the
walk delegates every disjointness verdict to LLVM's existing stack and
\emph{refuses} on anything unmodeled, the same posture as every other
import in \S\ref{sec:system-facts}.
\paragraph{Definitional encoding of stabilized values.}
Three successive countermodel inspections --- our standing diagnostic:
a should-prove query returning \textsc{sat} in microseconds indicates a
\emph{missing constraint}, not solver hardness --- exposed a chain of
encoding gaps, each closed by the same principle: replace a free
variable with the \emph{definitional axiom} of its defining code.
First, \texttt{freeze}: the vectorizer's multiversioning stabilizes its
guard flags against poison, and 13 of GEMM's 16 trap conditions were
freeze-wrapped --- encoded as unconstrained Booleans, their queries
were trivially satisfiable no matter what facts we supplied. Since
\texttt{freeze} is the identity on every non-poison operand, the axiom
$\den{\mathit{freeze}(x)} = \den{x}$
is sound over the encoder's over-approximation (a poisoned operand is
itself a free variable and can assume the frozen choice). Second,
trip-count bounds moved to \emph{subtraction form}:
\[
\den{\phi} - \mathit{start} \ \le_u\
s \cdot \mathrm{BTC}
\qquad\text{instead of}\qquad
\den{\phi} \ \le_u\ \mathit{start} + s \cdot
\mathrm{BTC},
\]
which makes the unit-stride upper bound unconditionally wrap-safe ---
modular subtraction cancels overflow exactly --- retiring the no-wrap
side conditions and admitting the \emph{symbolic} starts of remainder
loops for free. Third, the leaves of those bound expressions (the
vectorizer's resume indices and the preamble computing them) are
\emph{pre-encoded} through the ordinary instruction encoder: selects
become if-then-elses, merge-point phis become unions guarded by their
encoded branch predicates. No ``trip count $>0$'' is asserted anywhere;
the countermodels claiming to stand inside zero-trip loops die because
they violate the definitions. Every addition is true of all executions
by construction, so the over-approximation --- and hence soundness ---
is untouched. Together these took the GEMM acceptance test from 0 to
16-of-16 and, being in no way GEMM-specific, moved every sufficiently
similar benchmark (\S\ref{sec:frame-results}).

\subsection{Implementing Guard Synthesis}
\label{sec:guards-impl}
\tool{} attempts guard synthesis (pass parameters \texttt{mv} and
\texttt{narrow}) only for a trap whose direct query returned
\textsc{sat} or \textsc{unknown}, and only when the trap sits inside a
loop. A trap that is already proved dead never reaches this step, so
guard synthesis can add proofs but never lose one. It proceeds in five
steps (Algorithm~\ref{alg:guards}): generate candidate guards, verify
that the trap is dead under them, shrink them to a small guard, check
the guard for vacuity, and transform the loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Guard synthesis for one trap edge $(P, c)$ inside loop $L$}
\label{alg:guards}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require the direct query $\Gamma \wedge c$ returned \textsc{sat} or
  \textsc{unknown} \Comment{$\Gamma$: context, facts, guards}
\State $C \gets$ candidates from templates T1--T3 over values
  invariant in $L$ \Comment{generation}
\If{$\mathrm{check\mbox{-}sat}(\Gamma \wedge c \wedge \bigwedge C) \neq
  \textsc{unsat}$} \Comment{verification}
  \State \Return \textit{keep the trap}
\EndIf
\State $H \gets C \cap \mathrm{core}$ \Comment{a sufficient subset}
\ForAll{$h \in H$, upper bounds first, tightest first}
  \Comment{minimization}
  \If{$\mathrm{check\mbox{-}sat}(\Gamma \wedge c \wedge
    \bigwedge (H \setminus \{h\})) = \textsc{unsat}$}
    \State $H \gets H \setminus \{h\}$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State loosen each upper bound in $H$ while the query stays
  \textsc{unsat}
\If{$\mathrm{check\mbox{-}sat}(\Gamma \wedge \bigwedge H) =
  \textsc{unsat}$} \Comment{vacuity}
  \State \Return \textit{keep the trap}
\EndIf
\If{an operand of $H$ does not dominate the preheader of $L$}
  \State \Return \textit{keep the trap}
\EndIf
\State clone $L$ into $L_{\mathit{fast}}$; in the preheader, branch on
  $\bigwedge H$ \Comment{transformation}
\Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent} to $L_{\mathit{fast}}$ if it holds,
  to the original $L$ otherwise
\State in $L_{\mathit{fast}}$, fold the trap edge to its non-trap
  successor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Templates.} Every candidate is a comparison over values that
do not change inside the loop, so it can be evaluated once in the loop's
preheader. Three templates generate the candidates.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{T1, length versus index.} When the trap condition is
  $\mathit{idx} \ge_u \mathit{count}$, with $\mathit{count}$
  loop-invariant and $\mathit{idx}$ varying, propose
  $\mathit{count} >_u \mathit{hi}$, where $\mathit{hi}$ is the largest
  value the index can take according to its static unsigned range.
\item \textbf{T2, sane range.} For every loop-invariant integer input
  $v$, propose $0 \le_s v$ and a ladder of upper bounds $v \le_s 2^k$:
  far above realistic sizes, far below overflow.
\item \textbf{T3, symbolic index bound.} When the index is an affine
  function of the loop counters, compute its largest value over the
  whole loop nest from the loops' trip counts, and propose
  $\mathit{count} >_u \max(\mathit{idx})$. For \texttt{matmul!} this
  yields $|a| > n^2 - 1$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Verification.} All candidates are asserted together, each
tracked, on top of the context, the facts, and the trap condition. If
the solver answers \textsc{unsat}, the trap is dead under their
conjunction, and the unsat core names a sufficient subset $H$. If it
answers \textsc{sat} or \textsc{unknown}, no guard the templates can
express suffices, and the trap is kept.

\paragraph{Minimization and loosening.} Cores are not minimal. We drop
the conjuncts of $H$ one at a time, upper bounds first and tightest
first, keeping every drop that stays \textsc{unsat}. We then try to
relax each remaining upper bound, for example from $2^{15}$ to $2^{30}$,
while the query stays \textsc{unsat}. The result is a guard that holds
on as many real inputs as the proof allows.

\paragraph{Vacuity.} A guard that contradicts the context makes the trap
trivially unreachable, and the fast copy dead code. We therefore check
that $\Gamma \wedge H$ is satisfiable, and refuse the guard otherwise ---
the same self-distrust as the proof audit of \S\ref{sec:audit}.

\paragraph{Transformation.} Every operand of $H$ must dominate the
loop's preheader, so that $H$ can be evaluated there. The loop is
cloned; a branch on $H$ in the preheader selects the fast copy or the
original; in the fast copy, the proven trap branches are folded. Nested
loops are handled innermost first, each guard is hoisted to the
outermost loop in which it is invariant, and later clones inherit the
folds already made in earlier ones. When a verification query contains
64-bit multiplications that the solver struggles with, it is re-encoded
at 32 bits with explicit overflow flags; this is sound only under $H$,
which bounds the inputs, so it is used only to certify guards.

\subsection{Auditing the proofs}
\label{sec:audit}
A wrong proof is the one failure mode this tool must not have, so no
\textsc{unsat} is accepted at face value. Under \texttt{vacuity},
every \textsc{unsat} is re-solved with the trap condition removed: if
the context alone is already unsatisfiable --- an encoder bug, or a
genuinely contradictory guard chain --- the elimination is
\emph{refused} and reported. The audit is deliberately
self-distrusting: on zstd it surrendered 61 likely-sound eliminations
whose guard chains are jointly infeasible for the actual constants,
because the tool cannot distinguish that situation from its own bugs;
vacuous counts are reported in every static audit and are zero except
where noted. The timed builds run without this audit, for speed; guard
synthesis checks its own guards for vacuity in every build
(\S\ref{sec:guards-impl}). Independently, every assertion is tracked, so each
\textsc{unsat} yields a minimal core naming exactly the guards and
facts that proved it; we inspect the cores of every nontrivial proof
family by hand before counting it, and six standing \textsc{sat}
tripwire tests (may-alias clobbers, unrelated bounds) must stay
\textsc{sat} forever --- one flipping to \textsc{unsat} would mean a
fact source has become unsoundly strong.

\subsection{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
\paragraph{Machines.}
x86 measurements use a CloudLab~\cite{cloudlab} \texttt{c220g2} node:
dual Xeon E5-2660~v3 (Haswell-EP, 10 cores/socket, AVX2, no AVX-512),
DDR4-2133, Ubuntu 24.04, with Intel turbo disabled and every timed run
pinned to socket~0 (\texttt{numactl}). Apple-Silicon measurements use an
M-series MacBook Pro. The pass builds against pinned LLVM trunk
(23.0.0git) with Z3 4.8.12; Swift 6.3.3, Rust 1.97, Julia 1.12.
\paragraph{Timing protocol.}
Corpora live in tmpfs; every configuration is warmed; repetitions are
\emph{shuffled and interleaved} so drift spreads uniformly across
configurations; finals use 30 repetitions (20 for zlib; 21 rotated
repetitions for the Julia copies) and \emph{medians} are primary. Two controls isolate attribution: a
\texttt{base} binary (one verify round-trip through the identical
emit-IR/\texttt{opt}/\texttt{llc} sandwich) and a \texttt{base2x} binary
(the same full O3 applied \emph{twice}), so oracle-vs-\texttt{base2x}
deltas cannot be an artifact of extra conventional optimization exposure.
The \texttt{base}--\texttt{base2x} gap doubles as a per-run noise floor.
\paragraph{Honesty gates.}
Before any timing is trusted, all binaries must produce byte-identical
output on the workload; a mismatch aborts the experiment. Static
``eliminated'' counts are recomputed from the final IR, never inferred
from verdicts; crashes and timeouts are marked, never counted as
eliminations. Performance builds use a 10\,s per-query solver budget
and one solver thread, the budget of the library audits;
\S\ref{sec:results-cost} shows that \pct{96} of the proofs already
survive a 1\,s budget on zlib's \texttt{deflate.c}.
\paragraph{Benchmarks, specifications, tiers.}
Four C libraries (zlib, zstd, lz4, OpenSSL) under UBSan trap-mode
specifications; nine Swift kernels plus the unmodified CryptoSwift
library; Julia kernels including the stdlib GEMM of \S\ref{sec:frame};
and Rust ports that exercise the \texttt{traps=} retargeting. lz4 and
OpenSSL are static only: \tool{}'s lz4 build at the production budget
did not finish in reasonable time on the x86 node, and OpenSSL's signed
specification emits no checks. Dynamic
experiments use only specifications that do not fire at runtime --- a
firing specification (intentional wraparound in hashing code) is
reported as an un-runnable finding, never timed
(\S\ref{sec:results-perf}). Static results are reported at three fact
tiers (\emph{light}, \emph{heavy}, \emph{full} = heavy + frame,
\S\ref{sec:system-facts}). Every x86 runtime experiment uses one
configuration: the analysis facts and frame facts of the full tier, plus
inductive encoding and guard synthesis
(\texttt{heavy;frame;ind;mv;narrow}, 10\,s per query).

\end{itemize}

crypto code (CRC, Adler, xxHash, SHA) is intentionally-wrapping ---

OpenSSL's SHA-256 is the limit case: the signed spec emits \emph{zero}
checks (the code contains no instrumentable signed arithmetic), so under
strict UB there is simply nothing to eliminate. The split is dynamic as
well as static: a trap-mode \emph{unsigned} build of zstd's CLI executes
a trap in its first benchmark block --- the intentional wraps in its
xxhash checksum fire immediately --- so under that specification the
library is not merely check-laden but unrunnable, while the signed-spec
build runs cleanly, with checks costing $1.075\times$ in compression and
$1.002\times$ in decompression. Second, the \emph{affine
overfit}: PolyBench-style affine kernels emit zero residual traps at O3
--- LLVM's existing heuristics fully handle the affine case, and the
opportunities live in the data-dependent loops of compression and crypto.
Third, \emph{cores as evidence}: the nontrivial proofs' unsat cores name
2--4 fact sources plus guards (\S\ref{sec:conjunction}), and an ablation
of the shift encoding alone drops zlib's signed proofs from 2 to 0 and
unsigned from 41 to 25 --- the solver's exactness through shift
compositions is doing attributable work that range/known-bits reasoning
loses.

\subsection{RQ2: What is the source of speedup?}
\label{sec:results-mech}
A removed check pays exactly when it was blocking a further
transformation. A check is a side exit; inside a hot loop it prevents
unrolling and vectorization, and once it is gone the second
\texttt{-O3} performs them, so a few proofs can cascade into a large
speedup. SHA-256 is the typical case: seven proofs in the
message-schedule loop let it unroll, and the kernel runs $1.107\times$
faster on x86 ($1.074\times$ on Apple Silicon). Where the proofs land
matters more than how many there are: 264 scattered proofs and 112
guarded removals buy CryptoSwift nothing ($0.928\times$), while two
proofs and five guarded removals in base64's hot loop buy its entire
ceiling ($1.435\times$); 207 cold proofs leave zlib flat
($1.011\times$), while 16 on zstd's compression path buy $1.065\times$
of a $1.075\times$ ceiling.

Where a proof unlocks nothing, re-optimizing the simplified code is a
two-sided lottery. sha1 runs at $0.956\times$ on x86, replicated at
$0.954\times$ and at $0.95$--$0.96\times$ in four earlier runs, and the
adler32 proof that gains $1.031\times$ on x86 costs $0.985\times$ on
M-series. Swift lz77's $0.830\times$ is not caused by \tool{}: the
\texttt{-Ounchecked} build is equally slow ($0.831\times$). Conversely,
the ceiling is a reference point rather than a bound: on sha256, utf8,
and md5, \tool{}'s build beats \texttt{-Ounchecked}. The value of a
check varies in magnitude and in sign, and the microarchitecture is part
of the experiment.

\paragraph{Heap invariance.}
\label{sec:frame-results}
The largest single unlock is stdlib GEMM. With the frame facts of
\S\ref{sec:frame-machinery}, \tool{} proves all 16 of its trap edges,
where the scalar facts proved none; the flagship proof's unsat core
draws on six fact sources at once
($|\mathrm{SCEVSYM}|\,|\mathrm{KB}|\,|\mathrm{SCEVEQ}|{\times}3\,
|\mathrm{FRAME}|{\times}3$, plus four guards). Julia's JIT cannot
consume the pass output, so the proofs are deployed as proof-carrying
\texttt{@inbounds} annotations: annotating only the accesses proven at
measurement time gives $3.911\times$ of the $4.174\times$ all-annotated
ceiling on x86 ($4.156\times$ of $4.185\times$ on M-series), and the
proof-licensed build is faster at every one of 14 problem shapes, by
$1.5$--$6.8\times$ (Figure~\ref{fig:gemm-sweep},
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-full}).

\paragraph{Guard synthesis: which component pays?}
Most of the other large speedups --- Julia lz77 ($4.01\times$), base64
($1.44\times$), Rust matmul ($1.38\times$), Julia matmul, crc32 --- come
from guard synthesis and loop cloning (\S\ref{sec:guards}). Cloning a
loop behind a runtime test is not new (\S\ref{sec:relwork}): LLVM's own
inductive range-check elimination (IRCE) splits a loop around its range
checks and versions it behind a runtime range test. Guard synthesis has
two parts that could each be responsible for the gain: the
\emph{guard}, which the solver synthesizes and verifies, and the
\emph{proofs} that remove the checks inside the guarded copy. We
separate them with five pipelines that combine the two parts with
LLVM's counterparts:
(i)~\tool{} with guard synthesis: \tool{}'s guard and \tool{}'s proofs;
(ii)~\tool{}'s guard and loop copy, with every check left in place, so
that \texttt{-O3} must remove them: \tool{}'s guard, LLVM's proofs;
(iii)~IRCE followed by \tool{} without guard synthesis: LLVM's guard,
\tool{}'s proofs;
(iv)~IRCE followed by \texttt{-O3}: LLVM's guard and proofs; and
(v)~\texttt{-O3} alone. Every check is tagged with its own identity, so
each pipeline's removals can be compared check by check.

\emph{Pipeline (i) is the best or tied on every benchmark}, and the other
columns show why. On Julia lz77 and matmul, the two kernels where guard
synthesis pays most, nothing else removes a single check: without the
synthesized guard the checks are live, because nothing in the code ties
the arrays' lengths to the loop bounds (\S\ref{sec:guards}). Here the
guard is the whole speedup.
When the guard is handed to LLVM (ii), the result depends on how hard
the checks inside the copy are. On Rust matmul, \texttt{-O3} removes
both checks by itself once the guard is in place, and (ii) matches (i)
($1.372\times$ against $1.373\times$): the synthesized guard is what
pays, and LLVM's analyses suffice for the rest. IRCE's own guard, in
contrast, buys only $1.10\times$. On base64, the guard alone lifts
\texttt{-O3} to the level IRCE reaches ($1.26\times$ against
$1.25\times$), and \tool{}'s proofs inside the copy add the rest, up to
$1.44\times$: both halves are needed. On Julia sha256, only the
guarded removals separate (i) from the rest: every pipeline without
them lands at $1.02$--$1.03\times$, within run-to-run noise of each
other, while (i) reaches $1.089\times$.
crc32 is the counterexample: most of its gain comes from four checks
\tool{} proves dead outright, which it proves just as well after IRCE
(iii, $1.070\times$); the guard adds only the last $1.004\times$, and
IRCE alone removes nothing.
In aggregate, over all 385 checks of the 17 kernels (not only those in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), the five pipelines prove 85, 69, 52, 17, and
8 checks dead. Every check proved by (ii), (iv), or (v) is also proved
by (i); the single exception to nesting is (iii), which proves two GEMM
checks that (i) does not. The synthesized guard is therefore not a
repackaging of IRCE: IRCE's guard, even with \tool{}'s proofs behind
it, recovers at most part of what guard synthesis does, and on the
kernels where guard synthesis matters most it recovers nothing.

\paragraph{Compile cost.}
Table~\ref{tab:compile} prices three configurations on every benchmark
of Table~\ref{tab:speedups}. \emph{Thorough}, the configuration behind
every speedup, costs 1--13\,s per Swift and Rust kernel and 15--282\,s
per Julia kernel, against a conventional \texttt{-O3} compile of about
0.1\,s; the repositories carry the real cost: 715\,s for CryptoSwift and
2996\,s for zlib, 2277\,s of it in a single module (\texttt{trees.c}).
The two dials trade that cost for proofs. \emph{Fast} keeps 347 of
Thorough's 826 proofs (\pct{42}) at a seventh of zlib's cost, and
\emph{Light} keeps 178 (\pct{22}) at a sixtieth, with every kernel
under 2\,s in both. The proofs lost first are exactly the ones behind
the largest speedups: guard synthesis (base64 falls from 7 proofs to
0, crc32 from 11 to 0) and heap invariance (GEMM from 14 to 9 under
Fast and to 0 under Light). Cold, scattered proofs survive: Light still
keeps 80 of zlib's 289 and 27 of zstd's 82. The per-query budget bounds

milliseconds, and most of its 407\,s go to importing LLVM's analyses
and to guard synthesis, which the budget does not limit; Light, which

thread dial of Figure~\ref{fig:matrix} gives $3.7\times$ at 8 workers
and nothing more at 10 (Amdahl: serial discovery, fact import, and
guard synthesis).
\paragraph{Whole-compile cost.}
The ratio that matters to a developer is that of whole compiles, not of
one optimizer run. A build runs a front end, the optimizer, and code
generation, and the front end is often a large share of it: Swift's, for
instance, runs its own optimizer before LLVM sees the code. Comparing
against \texttt{-O3} alone would therefore overstate the cost. We timed
every stage of the two pipelines the runtime experiments use, per module,
on the 13 benchmarks whose pipeline can take the pass output (all but
Julia's): the baseline (optimizing front end, \texttt{-O3}, code
generation) and the same pipeline with \tool{} inserted before
\texttt{-O3}. Both come from one harness, so the comparison is like for
like. A whole compile with \tool{} costs a geometric mean of $8.3\times$
the baseline under Thorough (median $5.7\times$, range
$2.0$--$382\times$), $2.2\times$ under Fast (median $1.38\times$, range
$1.22$--$57\times$), and $1.5\times$ under Light (median $1.25\times$,
range $1.17$--$7.0\times$). The pass is nearly the whole difference: the
\texttt{-O3} and code-generation stages that follow it take at most about
\pct{16} longer than in the baseline, guarded loop copies included. The
top of every range is zlib, whose \texttt{trees.c} dominates
(Figure~\ref{fig:modules}).

\paragraph{Where each configuration fits.}
The repository totals overstate what a developer waits for. Builds
compile modules independently and rebuild only the ones that changed,
so the latency that matters in the edit--compile loop is per module, and
per module even Thorough is modest (Figure~\ref{fig:modules}): the median module takes 7.2\,s in
zlib and 2.4\,s in zstd, and 12 of zlib's 14 modules and 17 of zstd's
18 finish in under 75\,s. The modules that do the libraries' main work
fall in that range: zlib's compressor (\texttt{deflate.c}, 71\,s) and
decompressor (\texttt{inflate.c}, 64\,s), and zstd's Huffman encoder
(\texttt{huf\_compress.c}, 46\,s) and optimal parser
(\texttt{zstd\_opt.c}, 39\,s). The totals are set by a few outliers ---
\texttt{trees.c} (2277\,s) and \texttt{adler32.c} (530\,s) in zlib,
\texttt{zstd\_preSplit.c} (164\,s) in zstd --- whose heavily inlined
bodies give each query a long guard chain.

\emph{Fast} is the configuration for the development loop, and it is
both fast and effective. It compiles every kernel in under 2\,s (all
but one in under half a second) and the whole of zstd and of
CryptoSwift in 31\,s and 40\,s. Where it keeps Thorough's proofs it
keeps Thorough's speedups: on Rust matmul, adler32, sha1, and md5 it
removes exactly the checks Thorough removes, and it speeds up Rust
matmul by $1.336\times$ and adler32 by $1.039\times$, within run-to-run
variation of Thorough (Table~\ref{tab:fast},
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-full}). The exception is zlib's \texttt{trees.c},
which takes 407\,s even under Fast, for the fact-import cost discussed
above; Light compiles it in 34\,s.

We therefore recommend choosing the configuration by stage. During
development, compile whole repositories and large modules with Fast
(Light for an outlier such as \texttt{trees.c}), and the single module
being edited with Thorough, which per module stays between seconds and
about a minute. A wait of that order --- a few seconds for the typical
module, 7.2\,s and 2.4\,s at the median --- fits the edit--compile loop:
it is paid once per edit, only for the module that changed, while the
build reuses every other module's object file, and a module that is
still too slow for the developer's patience can be given a smaller
budget or compiled with Fast. Every configuration takes a flexible per-query budget,
and Fast and Light also scale with threads (Figure~\ref{fig:matrix});
Thorough runs on one thread because inductive encoding does. For the
final binary, compile the whole codebase with Thorough: a few minutes to
an hour, spent once on code that is no longer changing, buys the proofs
behind every speedup in Table~\ref{tab:speedups} --- the same trade
release builds already make with link-time and profile-guided
optimization. With this split, \tool{} stays an online pass during
development and becomes a thorough one at release.

23\,ms and \pct{92} take under a second, so a budget removes the

still (median 5.6\,ms), which matters because they are the large

\paragraph{The far end of the dial.}
OpenSSL's SHA-256, compiled with aggressive inlining, is the counterpoint:
each of its 2225 unsigned-spec queries carries the false-edges of up to
$\sim$200 \emph{preceding} overflow checks as dominating context. At
0.3--5\,s budgets the pass proves \emph{nothing} (all queries pinned at
the cap; identical for light and heavy tiers); at 30\,s, 43 proofs
appear; an uncapped historical run found 125. The budget curve
$0\,@\,1\text{s} \rightarrow 0\,@\,5\text{s} \rightarrow 43\,@\,30\text{s}
\rightarrow 125\,@\,\infty$ is monotone and tier-invariant: query hardness
here scales with inlined guard-chain depth, and the same knob that makes
zlib effectively free spans five orders of magnitude on real code. The
practical reading is favorable: a deployment at a 1\,s budget keeps
\pct{96} of zlib-class proofs and silently, soundly skips OpenSSL-class
queries (\textsc{unknown} keeps the check); an offline audit turns the
dial up.
\subsection{RQ4: How does super-analysis compare with superoptimization?}
\label{sec:results-souper}
We compare \tool{} with Souper~\cite{sasnauskas2017souper}, the
synthesizing superoptimizer for LLVM IR, on the task this paper targets:
removing the safety checks of the two C libraries. Souper runs in its
\emph{constant mode}, which only proves that a value is always the same
constant --- the mode its authors use as an online optimizer, and the one
that can fold a check whose condition is always false --- and, in a pilot,
in its CEGIS synthesis mode.

\paragraph{Setup.} Souper pins LLVM~18, whose parser cannot read the IR
of newer front ends, so the comparison covers the C libraries, compiled
with LLVM~18's clang. Each module is emitted \emph{once} and both tools
read the same file; \tool{}'s LLVM reads it as bitcode, which newer LLVM
versions are guaranteed to accept. Each tool runs with its own pinned
LLVM, built without assertions in both cases, and its own pinned Z3
(Souper: Z3 4.13, as an external process per query; \tool{}: Z3 4.8.12,
in process), is followed by its own LLVM's \texttt{-O3}, and is compared
against that LLVM's \texttt{-O3} alone. Souper's per-query budget is
10\,s, as for Thorough, with no external cache; its in-memory cache of
identical queries is left on, as shipped. Swift, Rust, and Julia are
excluded because their front ends emit IR newer than LLVM~18.

in each of which Thorough removes at least as many, and the two pipelines
end in different LLVM versions, so a runtime difference would mostly
measure LLVM~18 against LLVM~23 rather than the two tools.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relwork}
\S\ref{sec:related} placed super-analysis among the paradigms of
aggressive optimization; here we compare it with work that shares its
tools or its target.

\paragraph{SMT for compiler analysis.}
Closer in spirit to our work is Taneja et al.~\cite{taneja2020knownbits},
who use an SMT solver to test LLVM's dataflow analyses. For each expression they compute the
\emph{most precise} answer an analysis could possibly give --- the
exact set of known bits, the tightest integer range --- and compare it
with LLVM's. LLVM's answers are sound but often imprecise: only \pct{48.7} of its integer ranges and
\pct{35.0} of its demanded-bits results are as tight as possible. Their solver-based analysis
is slow --- 9\,s per expression for known bits, 16\,s for integer
ranges --- and they conclude it is ``too slow to use at compile time'', and is not viable as an online optimizer.
The cost comes from the question they ask. Finding the best abstract
value is a \emph{search} over the abstract domain: known bits takes
$2n$ solver queries for an $n$-bit value, and ranges take a binary
search, so one answer costs dozens of queries. \tool{} asks a different
and much cheaper question: one yes/no reachability query per check
site (decision rather than search), which is why our median query
takes milliseconds (\S\ref{sec:results-cost}).
Alive2~\cite{lopes2021alive2} applies a solver to compiler
\emph{correctness} rather than precision: it validates LLVM's
transformations by checking refinement between the IR before and after
each pass. Like \tool{}, it treats unencoded behavior as an
over-approximation, so a solver verdict is only trusted in one
direction.

\paragraph{Bounds-check elimination.}
Removing redundant array bounds checks is a classic problem.
ABCD~\cite{bodik2000abcd} proves checks redundant on demand by solving
a system of difference constraints over an extended SSA form, and
production JIT compilers such as HotSpot's client
compiler~\cite{wurthinger2007hotspot} eliminate checks with range
analysis and by hoisting them out of loops. LLVM's inductive range-check
elimination splits a loop into a pre-loop, a check-free main loop, and
a post-loop when the checked index is an affine function of the
induction variable. Each of these reasons in a single abstract domain
chosen in advance; the proofs \tool{} needs combine several domains at
once (\S\ref{sec:conjunction}) and, for heap-resident lengths, memory
(\S\ref{sec:frame}).

\paragraph{Reducing sanitizer overhead.}
Sanitizers such as AddressSanitizer~\cite{serebryany2012asan} and
UBSan~\cite{ubsanDocs} are the most widely deployed runtime checks.
ASAP~\cite{wagner2015asap} lowers their cost by removing the checks on
the hottest code, trading safety for speed under a user-chosen overhead
budget; \tool{} removes only checks it proves can never fire, so the
safety guarantee is unchanged. SanRazor~\cite{zhang2021sanrazor}
removes checks that are redundant with other checks, using a
combination of static and dynamic (profiling) analysis; \tool{}'s
removals are static and each is backed by a solver proof.

\paragraph{Guards and loop versioning.}
Cloning a loop behind a runtime test is established practice: .NET's
RyuJIT clones loops to remove bounds checks, and LLVM's loop versioning
guards a copy by runtime alias checks. In both, the guard is taken from a
fixed syntactic pattern. Guard synthesis (\S\ref{sec:guards}) instead
obtains the guard from the solver, as an abductive
hypothesis~\cite{dillig2012abductive,dillig2013abductive}, and verifies
that it is sufficient and consistent before the loop is cloned.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We introduced super-analysis, an alternative paradigm of aggressive compilation which uses a solver for analysis only. We show that this paradigm is effective: it can speedup our benchmarks up to $4.01\times$ on x86. Super-analysis can be used online, and the cost of compilation can be controlled by parallelization and strict SMT timeout budgets. Our implementation, \tool{} targeted safety check removal and loop cloning under synthesized guards. Future work consists of extending this paradigm into other analyses and optimizations.

\section{Residue: What the Current Fact Sources Cannot Prove}
\label{sec:future}
The checks \tool{} leaves behind are not a random remainder. They fall
into four classes, each defined by the \emph{kind} of fact that is
missing rather than by the benchmark it appears in. The last column of
Table~\ref{tab:frontier} labels each unrecovered ceiling with its class.

\paragraph{(a) Symbolic trip counts --- solved.}
A loop counter bounded by an SSA-valued expression rather than a
constant. Dominance-based guard collection cannot see the bound, because
the latch that establishes it does not dominate the check. The
\textsc{scevsym} fact source (\S\ref{sec:system-facts}) supplies it:
umin-exact for single-exit loops, a symbolic maximum for multi-exit
loops, exact division for strided counts. This class is closed.

\paragraph{(b) Heap and interprocedural length invariants.}
The length that bounds an index lives in memory, not in a register:
Swift's \texttt{array.count} on a runtime allocation, Rust's \texttt{Vec}
length behind opaque provenance, Julia's array-header \texttt{size}
field on an argument. Each load of it is a fresh SMT variable, so a
guard that constrains one load says nothing about the next. Heap
invariance (\S\ref{sec:frame}) closes the intraprocedural case where the
two loads read a provably unclobbered location --- this is what takes
stdlib GEMM from 0 to 16 of 16 --- but the interprocedural and
call-carried cases remain, and they own the largest unrecovered
ceilings in Table~\ref{tab:frontier}.

\paragraph{(c) Anchor gaps --- solved.}
One check site reached by several predecessors, or several sites sharing
one error block, so that a single query has to hold on paths with
incompatible contexts. Splitting the obligation per incoming edge, and
allowing partial elimination of a shared error block, closes it.

\paragraph{(d) Non-unit and variable stride induction.}
Scalar evolution computes no trip count at all, in any form, for a loop
whose latch compares an unsigned counter advancing by a stride greater
than one --- base64 at stride 3, crc32 at stride 4, UTF-8 validation at
a data-dependent stride of 1 to 4. No amount of trip-count machinery
helps, because there is no trip count to translate. The remedy is to
stop asking for one: encode the induction structurally, as a base case
plus a step case over a copy of the loop body
(\S\ref{sec:app-machinery}). That closes crc32's four
stride-4 bounds checks; base64 and UTF-8 remain, the former because its
residue is also class~(b). Guard synthesis (\S\ref{sec:guards}) reaches
base64's hot checks instead, in a guarded copy of the loop.

\subsection{Prediction record}
Each mechanism's prediction was written before its code. Outcomes:
PHIINV --- Swift lz77 $0 \to 5$ and lz77.jl $0 \to 4$ as predicted.
Multi-versioning --- base64 folds and lz77.jl folds as predicted;
predicted CryptoSwift folds obtained. Narrowing --- matmul.jl 3/3 only
after the unpredicted linearization step; the monotonicity-lemma
variant was tried, did not help, and was removed. FRAME~v2 (unifying
Julia size reloads across blocks) --- premise falsified: the reloads
carry no alias metadata and MemorySSA reports a clobber; abandoned.
Inductive encoding --- predicted 13/13 recovered with rules off,
observed 12/13 plus one further miss (Rust lz77); the shared cause is
the auxiliary \emph{hx} invariant. Cross-level composition facts ---
predicted to recover both misses, falsified (composition was not the
blocker), mechanism removed. Pointer-$\phi$ linking --- predicted no
gain because pointers are havoced; held. Out-of-sample sweep ---
predicted $+5$ to $+30$ on CryptoSwift and $\approx 0$ on C libraries;
observed $+43$ on CryptoSwift and $+11$ on zstd (falsified upward),
$0$ on zlib and lz4 (held); superset property held on every corpus.
